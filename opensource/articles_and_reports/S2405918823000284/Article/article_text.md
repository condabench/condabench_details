IntroductionThis research examines out-of-sample portfolio optimization by improving the ex-ante conditional expectations of risk and returns that optimize mean-variance portfolio selection. The tests cover 52 years of data over various economic cycles. One common, yet difficult-to-answer question in financial economics is, why do some machine learning algorithms improve asset allocation and portfolio performance while others do not? For some machine learning methodologies, reasons for performance improvement are nearly impossible to answer given the level of complexity and lack of interpretability of the models. For other machine learning algorithms, it is possible to gain insight into the abnormal returns question. With the more interpretable models, it is also important to minimize complexity by simplifying the inputs and outputs. We contribute to the literature by providing a machine learning model that both improves adjusted-stock performance and is also understandable to investors by employing hierarchical clustering.Our analysis builds on the work of Lopez de Prado (2016) and conducts a performance analysis of the excess returns from a portfolio that has been optimized after clustering the covariance matrix with an expanded number of stocks over a time series. A simple way to conceptualize the clustering algorithm (referred to as the “cluster-optimized portfolio” hereafter) is that it conditions the covariance matrix on the correlations of included firms, which generates optimized weights relative to an unconditional covariance estimate. We compare the performance of the cluster-optimized portfolio to that of a traditional Markowitz portfolio and a market portfolio. The analysis consists of decomposing the characteristics of each portfolio to understand what drives the difference in performance. To assess how, why, and when portfolio performance improves across each method, we analyze the excess return, risk, and risk exposure using five risk-factor models (Capital Asset Pricing Model, Fama-French 3-factor model, Carhart 4-factor model, Fama-French 5-factor model, and the Fama-French 5-factor + Momentum model), and Sharpe-ratios with both time series and cross-section panels. Additionally, we specifically examine portfolio performance during market downturns, something that has, according to our knowledge, yet to be documented in this type of setting using hierarchical clustering. Comparing three-month buy-and-hold, long-only strategies to a cross-section of stocks on three major U.S. exchanges (NYSE, Amex, and Nasdaq) we find that by conditioning the covariance matrix based on a hierarchical clustering model, we statistically and economically improve out-of-sample return, risk, and Sharpe-ratio performance relative to the traditional Markowitz and market portfolios. The improvements are achieved by elevating the average return of the portfolio while maintaining a similar level of risk relative to the two benchmarks. Moreover, we find that the improved performance of the cluster-optimized portfolio is not subsumed by high portfolio rebalancing costs, rather, the cluster-optimized portfolio has significantly lower average portfolio rebalancing than the Markowitz portfolio, during all economic business cycles.2. Literature and motivationIt is well known that the traditional Markowitz approach to portfolio allocation suffers from high investment turnover, poor out-of-sample performance, and unbalanced weight allocation (Markowitz, 1952; Black and Litterman, 1992; Fabozzi et al., 2002; Lopez de Prado, 2016). Our long-only strategy is motivated by Jagannathan and Ma (2003) whose findings document that when portfolio weights are constrained to be nonnegative, the optimal portfolios experience a reduction in risk. However, since short selling is costly and not implementable by many institutions or other market participants, we focus on an investment strategy that is constrained to take only long positions.The existing literature documents that hierarchical clustering improves minimum-variance allocation and improves risk-adjusted returns over short time periods, or to a specific cross-section of stocks (e.g., S&P 500 companies only). For example, Leon et al. (2017) document that hierarchical clustering algorithms achieve the best financial performance between returns and the Omega Ratio risk-adjustment factor relative to classical mean-variance optimization and other clustering techniques. Their analysis consists of a sample of 175 stocks from the Russell 1000 over a two-month period using 1-min return data. Tola et al. (2008) present findings that the correlation matrix can improve portfolio performance under the assumption of perfect forecasting of future returns and volatility by applying simple clustering methods (see also Laloux et al. (2000); Rosenow et al. (2002); Zhang and Maringer (2009)). The work of Lohre et al. (2012) shapes our analysis by focusing on a specific type of risk parity strategy for portfolio selection utilizing the correlation of stock returns, and we extend the literature on applying new data science method in portfolio optimization (Alexander et al. (2023); Menvouta et al. (2023)).Extending the previously mentioned research, we study the long-term performance of hierarchical clustering when investments are spread out across a large cross-section of stocks over long investment horizons. We analyze portfolio performance over varying economic conditions and take an investment approach to analyze the risk of each portfolio type. We find significant and economically meaningful evidence that, on average, by utilizing hierarchical clustering to estimate the covariance matrix and allocation weights, investors can improve their long-term investment performance by earning higher risk-adjusted returns with lower average portfolio turnover.3. Data and methodologyThe data for this paper uses monthly returns of common stocks listed on the three major U.S. stock exchanges (NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ), as reported by the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) from 1965 to 2017. We note that the heighted geopolitical tensions affecting trade (Martin and Wang, 2019) and the unexpected economic disruptions initiated by the Covid-19 pandemic that began to materialize in late 2018 introduced non-trivial anomalies in financial datasets (Fernandez et al., 2020; Turner, 2020; Williams, 2020). Additionally, Lee and Zhao (2019) document that global economic uncertainties, typified by escalating trade tensions between major economies, led to a notable change in stock price volatility. Thus, to gain insights from a more stable pre-regulation and pre-pandemic period, we conclude the data sample in 2017.Stocks with a price below $5, below the lowest decile of NYSE size breakpoints, and above the highest quintile of the Amihud illiquidity measure are removed to help limit potential liquidity constraints. Data filters are applied throughout the entire sample prior to any model estimation. There are a total of 1,437,127 observations with 17,289 unique stocks. Table 1 reports the summary statistics for the average number of stocks included for each look-back period. We obtain data on market excess returns, the risk-free rate, size (SMB), value (HML), profitability (RMW), investment (CMA), and momentum (MOM) factors from Ken French's data library (See Carhart (1997), Fama and French (1993); Jegadeesh and Titman (1993); Litner (1965); Sharpe (1964), and http://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html for additional information). We use the model presented by Markowitz (1952) as the primary benchmark, a canonical method in modern portfolio theory. We adopt a three-month buy-and-hold investment strategy to reduce transaction costs and excessive rebalancing of portfolios. Additionally, while zero-cost portfolio weights can be estimated, we focus solely on a long-only investment strategy, i.e., we implement a short-selling constraint.Table 1. Clustering the covariance matrixHierarchical clustering is a clustering method that builds a hierarchy of relationships among observations (e.g., stocks) based on their correlations. Hierarchical clustering is documented to render the best results when dealing with stocks (Leon et al., 2017). In the first step, we compute the correlation matrix and encode it as a distance matrix D. We calculate a distance matrix conditional on the correlations between the stocks in the cross-section at a given time. We use a standard distance metric,(1)Distance=12(1−ρi,j)where ρi,j is the correlation between assets i and j, and 0 if i = j. We then use the distance matrix to create a hierarchy based on the distance between the correlations of each stock. The distance is minimized between each stock, that is, the stocks that are highly correlated with each other are located close to each other in the hierarchy, whereas stocks that are not highly correlated with each other will be further apart. The distance matrix is then used to estimate the clustered covariance matrix. Fig. 1 represents a simplified, illustrative example using 10 randomly selected stocks. We show the difference between the unclustered and clustered covariance matrices, as well as the weight assigned to each asset by the respective approach. The covariance matrix is heat-mapped by the correlation of the assets. There is a clear distinction between the two covariance matrices and their respective weight allocations. Instead of taking all returns and allocating investments randomly to portfolios, the assets are first clustered by their correlations, and then capital is allocated conditioned on the covariance of the clustered assets. A great resource for a more in-depth explanation is found in Advances in Financial Machine Learning by Marcos Lopez de Prado.

Additional constraints can be imposed according to investor preference.When clustering the assets by correlations, the investment strategy is enhanced by reducing the amount of risk (or better-diversifying investments) based on how assets covary in clusters, not just random covariation. Clustering also enhances the reliability of the out-of-sample performance of the portfolio (Tola et al., 2008). Note that the Markowitz portfolio is subjected to idiosyncratic risk because of its concentration in only some of the investments while ignoring others completely. By clustering, some of the idiosyncratic risk is mitigated by diversifying across all assets and across the cluster. Lopez de Prado (2016) documents the performance improvement by implementing these methodologies when searching for the minimum-variance portfolio that maximizes out-of-sample performance. We apply a similar methodology to maximize out-of-sample, risk-adjusted returns over a long time series that includes several business cycles. Using the clustered covariance matrix, we optimize portfolio allocations as follows,(2)w∈argmaxwwtE[R]−rfwtΣws.t.,wt1=1andwi=[0,1],where w is a vector of weights that sum to 1 and each wi is bound between 0 and 1, E[R] is a vector of expected returns, rf is the risk-free rate, Σ is the covariance matrix of returns, and 1 is a vector of ones.Additionally, we examine why, characteristically and economically, there is a performance improvement when clustering the covariance matrix in the Markowitz framework by studying the components of the portfolio excess returns and its risk exposure. Without clustering, Markowitz's weights give us today's optimal portfolio. As portfolio managers and investors, the ideal portfolio will not be the one that maximizes today's risk-adjusted returns, but rather the portfolio that maximizes the next period's risk-adjusted returns. Thus, we can think of the clustered covariance matrix as being conditioned on the correlation of stocks, which gives a better approximation of how investments should be allocated to maximize the next period's performance.Our first hypothesis (H1) is that the cluster-optimized portfolio will attain a superior approximation of where to allocate investments such that we achieve higher out-of-sample risk-adjusted returns over a long time series relative to the Markowitz portfolio because by incorporating more information into the covariance matrix, we achieve better approximations for next period's optimal allocation.H1The portfolios estimated conditioned on hierarchical clustering will outperform the unconditional Markowitz portfolio and the market portfolio.Our second hypothesis (H2) is that the reduction in risk is due to an inherent change in the risk exposure relative to the Markowitz portfolio. This risk reduction is because the cluster-optimized portfolio is a standard portfolio with asset tilts. The difference here is that the machine learning algorithm is automating the optimal weight tilts. Therefore, instead of having to know and understand each available stock and then manually guessing optimal tilts that improve my portfolio's performance next period, we are able to use mathematical properties to automate an optimally-tilted portfolio.H2The performance improvement of the cluster-optimized portfolio using hierarchical clustering is driven by changes in risk exposure relative to the Markowitz portfolio and market portfolio.Our third hypothesis (H3) is that the cluster-optimized portfolio will achieve higher, more stable alphas, on average. This is attributed to the use of machine learning – we create a portfolio using information that is not easily detected by the average market participant. Additionally, due to its statistical nature, the optimized portfolio creates a type of empirical arbitrage.H3The cluster-optimized portfolio will outperform the Markowitz portfolio and market portfolio by earning higher abnormal returns.Our final hypothesis (H4) results from the prior discussion and argues that the cluster-optimized portfolio is able to reduce average losses during market downturns with higher average Sharpe-ratios.H4The cluster-optimized portfolio will reduce the downside loss during poor economic markets such as the financial crisis.4. Empirical tests and resultsWe find that by clustering the covariance matrix conditioned on the correlations among stocks, we are able to accomplish improved out-of-sample portfolio performance on a risk-adjusted basis when exposed to a much larger basket of stocks that expands over several different types of market conditions compared to an unconditional Markowitz portfolio and market portfolio.In Table 2, we analyze the average monthly excess portfolio returns, the standard deviation of excess returns, and the average difference in Sharpe-ratios for the cluster-optimized, Markowitz, and market portfolios using 12-, 60-, and 120-month look-back windows to estimate the covariance matrix. Panel A reports results for a 12-month window. The cluster-optimized portfolio earns statistically and economically higher risk-adjust returns, on average. The performance improvement is relatively different when compared to the Markowitz portfolio or market portfolio. Compared to the market, the cluster-optimized portfolio closely matches the level of risk while significantly improving excess return performance. The opposite is true when compared to the Markowitz portfolio. On average, the excess returns of the Markowitz portfolio are higher, but the risk exposure of the cluster-optimized portfolio is significantly lower with an average standard deviation of 4.84 % versus the 7.47 % of Markowitz. On average, the Sharpe-ratios for the cluster-optimized portfolio are approximately 3 % higher than the market and Markowitz portfolios, significant at the 1 % level.Table 2. ConclusionWe provide significant and robust evidence of improved investment performance by conditioning portfolio allocation using hierarchical clustering. Out-of-sample portfolio performance is studied using a large cross-section of U.S. traded stocks for over fifty years. The cluster-optimized portfolio formed using hierarchical clustering is found to have statistically and economically meaningful performance improvements relative to two benchmarks, the market portfolio and Markowitz's portfolio selection approach. The results hold true in a series of robustness tests that include the 25-size/book-to-market portfolios. Evidence that hierarchical clustering improves portfolio allocation is not new, however an investment study over a long time-series and cross-section of stocks is.Understanding how machine learning algorithms improve predictions about markets, specifically portfolio allocation, is vitally important to academics and investors alike because ensuring that results are not spurious, understanding where performance improvement thrives and falls short, and understanding the optimal implementation of techniques can only be accomplished through rigorous study of how, why, and where improvements are made. We contribute to the financial economics and machine learning literature by providing a robust analysis of portfolio performance when using a conditional covariance matrix (correlation-clustered covariance matrix) versus an unconditional covariance matrix (traditional Markowitz portfolio) using a 3-month buy-and-hold, long-only strategy. We are also one of the first to analyze the characteristics of each portfolio by studying the excess return, risk, average Sharpe-ratios, risk exposure, portfolio characteristic differences, and economic cycle performance in one paper. We document that machine learning improves ex-ante conditional expectations of risk that increase out-of-sample portfolio performance and that the performance improvement is due to shifts in risk exposure and higher average portfolio returns.Declaration of competing interestNone.Recommended articlesReferencesAlexander et al., 2023N.
