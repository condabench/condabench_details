IntroductionThe diverse influences of education on economic growth are extensively studied, namely the accumulation of qualified human capital (in terms of training, maintenance, knowledge and workforce.). Education is considered as the base of the country's technological development. The latter is treated as an index measuring the degree of development of a given country in comparison to others. This index is also called the ‘technological frontier’ according to the classification adopted by the United States.It is important to note that public investment in the education sector and in Research and Development (R&D) represents a heavy burden on the government budget. At the same time, it is an essential element in stimulating innovation, creativity and technological development. Generally, countries with an advanced technological level have better opportunities to dominate the international market. In fact, they manage to increase their exports thanks to the modern means and techniques that enable them to improve their productivity.As for developing and poor countries, most of them have not yet reached an acceptable level in the field of education. These countries suffer from a high illiteracy rate as well as a very low youth literacy rate (as a percentage of people aged 15–24) compared to advanced countries. According to the Statistical Institute of the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO), there are 53,04568 illiterates person between the age of 14 and 24 in Bangladesh, 65,34263 illiterates person in Ethiopia, and 24277115 illiterates person in India. By contrast, there are only 245 illiterates person in Cyprus and 1240 illiterates person in Croatia.Therefore, the least-developed countries with high illiteracy rates are called upon to invest heavily in the education sector with the aim of preparing future generations better taught and more able to create and innovate. However, investment in the education sector requires enormous sources of funding. Generally, developed countries are the largest education spenders in the world in terms of the share of national wealth devoted to the financing of the education sector. In 2017, education absorbed 8.2% of the national wealth produced in Denmark and 7.6% in Sweden; By contrast, Myanmar and Chad devoted only 1.8% and 2.8% of their GDP to education, respectively (UNDP 2018 and World Bank 2018).Lucas1 evaluated the participation of human capital in economic growth by presenting an additional role of human capital as a driving force to attract other factors of production. The study suggests that developing countries must invest in higher education; to produce technicians, professionals and administrative workers. Lucas gives more importance to tertiary education, but he has not shown the impact of primary education on economic growth.Bassanini and Scarpetta2 used a panel of 21 OECD countries for the period 1971–1998 to study the effects of human capital, R&D, population growth, and investment on real GDP per capita. They found that the years of schooling and the total R&D spending have a significantly positive effect on the per capita GDP growth rate. On his part, Dao3 studied 28 developing countries and demonstrated the positive relationship between economic progress and the growth rate of public spending. Musila and Belassi4 used the time series method to examine the relationship between public spending on education per capita and economic growth in Uganda during the period 1965–1999. The empirical results show that education spending per worker has a significantly positive impact on economic growth both in the long and short terms. The correction model error estimates suggest that a 1% increase in education spending per worker on average will lead to about 0.04% increase in the output in the short term. Estimates of co-integration show that a 1% increase in education spending per worker on average will increase the output by about 0.6% in the long term. Aghion et al5 studies the relationship between technological change and education policies using data from OECD countries and 50 United States. The study concluded that higher education is the main cause of the divergence economy. But it is not only the years of schooling that count, but the quality of education is important for economic growth.Blankenau et al,6 examined the correlation between long-term education spending and economic growth and find that there are positively related based on a sample of 23 developed countries. However, this relationship is sensitive to fiscal constraint imposition. They explained the inability of some studies to find a significant relationship between spending targets for public education and growth by a failure to properly account for the funding method.Zeira7 concluded that if growth is driven by the industrialization of production, where machines replace the workforce in a growing number of tasks, then the exploitation of these machines requires that workers who are trained, namely to read and write and know arithmetic, whose human capital is less specific and more general. As a result, the adoption of technology is negatively dependent on the wages of skilled workers. Therefore, economic growth depends negatively on the cost of education. The econometric model used by Zeira shows that if the cost of education is high, economic growth could be slow and even stop altogether, eventually creating a development trap.Abdellatif et al,8 examined the effect of the link between education spending and disbursements as a source of international capital flows and investment on economic growth in Philippine Islands. The results show that there is a positive relationship between the rate of economic growth, payments, and education spending.2. The ARDL econometric modelThe study draws on the ARDL approach that is proposed by Pesaran et al9 and subsequently it was modified by Pesaran, Shin and Smith10 by introducing the bounds testing approches. We select this technique for two main reasons: First, it is effective in executing the short- and long-term relationships between the different variables that do not have the same order of integration - provided that such variables are stationary in level; I (0), and/or they are stationary in the first difference; I (1). Second, the ARDL approach can remove the problems associated with omitted variables and auto-correlation. Third, it can be useful for a small sample size application.10 The model used for the application of the ARDL approach draws on the theory of human capital discussed by Schultz11 and Becker12 and is based on the education of individuals and their investment choices in education and training during the cycle of their lives. This idea was introduced by Lucas13 in the model of endogenous growth. The accumulation of human capital is an essential factor in the process of economic growth and the rate of catch-up of developing countries. With the intention of studying the relationship between public spending on education and economic growth in Tunisia and Morocco, Solow's growth model14 is used as a theoretical framework. The model shows that national production (Y) is determined by three factors: capital (K), labor (L), and technical progress (A). The production function takes the following form:(1)Y=Af(K,L)In most cases, the Solow model is expressed as a Cobb–Douglas production function that takes the following form:(2)Y=AKαL1−αFor α ∈[0,1], indicating constant returns to scale.To make equation (2) linear, we apply the logarithmic function and we introduce an error term (Ɛ). The equation takes this form:(3)Ln(Y)=Ln(A)+αLn(K)+(1−α)Ln(L)+ƐAssuming that the technical progress (A) is being a residue and substituting of (K) for public education expenditure and (L) for education {GERPF,GERPMandSLE}; the equation (3) will be estimated in the following form:(4)Ln(Y)=α0+α1Ln(K)+α2Ln(L)+ƐWhere α0>0; α1>0 and α2>0 and Y shows GDP per capita.Introducing our variables, the model becomes as follows:(5)lnGDPper capita(t)=α0+β1lnexpenditureeducation(t)+β2GERFPE(t)+β3GERMPE(t)+β4SLE(t)+β5INFLATION(t)+Ɛ(t)The equation of the ARDL model is written as follows:(6)DlnGDPpercapitat=α0+∑i=1pγiDlnGDPpercapitat−i+∑i=1pβ1lnGDPpercapitat−1+∑i=1qδiDlneducationexpendituret−i+∑i=1qβ2lneducationexpendituret−1+∑i=1qθiDGERFPEt−i+∑i=1qβ3GERFPEt−1+∑i=1qϑiDGERMPEt−i+∑i=1qβ4GERMPEt−1+∑i=1qμiDSLEt−i+∑1qβ5SLEt−1+∑i=1qπiDINFLATIONt−i+∑i=1qβ6INFLATIONt−1+εtWhere, D is the delay operator; is the constant; mean short-term dynamics; while: are the long-term coefficients and Ɛ is the error term of the white noise.After regression of Equation (5), the Wald test (F-statistic) was used to identify the existence of long-run relationship between the different variables. The Wald test for multiple parameters can be carried out by imposing restrictions on the estimated long-run coefficients of GDP per capita with the different variables of the model. According to the results shown in Table 2, the F-statistic value shows that it is significant at 10%, so the long-run null hypothesis (non-cointegration) is rejected. Robustness checkThe CUSUM and CUSUMSQ tests are frequently indicated in graphic form Fig. 4, Fig. 5 as below. The value of the statistic must then be verified under the null hypothesis that indicates the stability of the relationship curve in an interval (Bresson and Pirotte, 1995) fixed by two straight lines. In the case of a time series, the stability tests, also called structural change tests, examine the stability of the estimated coefficients of the equation while showing the presence of a structural change in the correlation. The CUSUM test is then adapted to test the hypothesis of stability of the long-run relations between GDP per capita and its determinants, in particular public spending on education. For Morocco, it is noted that the curves of the CUSUM and CUUMSQ tests are within the limits of the confidence interval at the 5% threshold. This result shows that the model chosen to estimate the relationships between the public educational expenditure and economic growth as well as the other variables is stable. As for Tunisia, it can be seen that the CUSUMSQ route exits on two occasions outside the limits of the significance interval at the 5% threshold. As a result, the coefficients appear to be unstable between 1997-2006 and 2010–2014. The first model break was in 1997, showing the instability of the coefficients of the economic variables used; and subsequently refers to the instability of the Tunisian economic state.

ConclusionThe objective of this paper is to study the impact of public education spending on GDP per capita in the case of Tunisia and Morocco. Our study is based on the proposals of the doctrines of human capital and endogenous growth to ensure investment in the educational sector as a means for the development of the nation. Education is therefore an essential factor for economic, social and even political change and transition. The results show that all the variables used have signs of the coefficients common to both countries, except the variable INFLATION. Public spending on education has a positive impact on GDP per capita of both countries, but more intensively so in Morocco. This result is expected since investment in the education sector makes it possible to improve the index of education in particular and human development in general - major factors for development, according to Lucas.13 Education offers people the opportunity to broaden their knowledge and hone their skills. It is also a fundamental incentive for research, development and innovation as well as the accumulation of human capital and skilled labor, which are decisive factors for growth. In other words, they serve to stimulate productivity and accelerate economic development. This finding was reached by Diamond15 and Villanueva (1994).16We note that GERFPE has negative effects on the Tunisian and Moroccan economy. This result appears unexpected, but a thorough study of the causes gives us shocking results. According to UNESCO (2016), more than 62% of Tunisian girls of primary school age are out of school in 2009; this rate reaches 54% in Morocco in 2014. As a result, a significant proportion of the population does not have the chance or opportunity to progress and improve their skills; and subsequently, there is a massive loss of human capital and labor input. Therefore, the Tunisian and Moroccan governments are called upon to formulate strategies that combat this social, and even cultural, problem in order to better exploit their human resources. As for the GERMPE variable, it is used to increase the GDP per capita in both countries. This result also confirms the important role of education in stimulating economic growth.Life expectancy has a positive effect on national economic development for both countries but this effect is more effective in Morocco. It can be said that the improved index of life expectancy at birth will result in an increase in saving, which positively affects the stock of capital. Accordingly, an increased stock of capital causes an increase in the volumes of investment causing, in their turn, the creation of a movement of labor demand and the amplification of production.Finally, we note that inflation has negative impact on the Tunisian GDP per capita. This can be explained by the effects of the increase of the general price level which affects the purchasing power of individuals negatively. Therefore, total household consumption will reduce resulting in a decline in aggregate demand. Accordingly, production will be reduced. The decline in output leads to a fall in demand for labor, which means an increase in the unemployment rate. In Morocco, the result is different as inflation is found to have a positive impact on GDP per capita.
