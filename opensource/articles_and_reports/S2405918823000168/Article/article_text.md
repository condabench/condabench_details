IntroductionThe Chinese corporate bond market has experienced dramatic growth in recent years. The outstanding amount of Chinese corporate bond increased from 1.26 trillion RMB in 2008 to 31.13 trillion RMB in 2022, accounting for 22% of China's total bond outstanding. With the progress of deregulation and liberalization in China's onshore capital market, Chinese corporate bond has become increasingly important for investment from both domestic and international investors. Therefore, understanding which factors drive Chinese corporate bond returns is critical to investors' decisions.Unlike most countries, China has two distinct bond markets: the over-the-counter (OTC) interbank market, and the centralized exchange market. The interbank market and the exchange market differ in several aspects such as trading mechanisms, bond types, regulatory supervisors, clearing and depository companies, and market investors. For instance, the major participants in the interbank market are institutions especially commercial banks, whereas the participants in the exchange market include non-bank institutions and retail investors.1 These differences can limit cross-market arbitrage activities and result in severe market segmentation (Amstad and He, 2019; Chen et al., 2020). Given these distinctions, it is natural to ask whether bond characteristics have different predictive patterns for corporate bond returns in the interbank market and the exchange market. For example, Liu et al. (2019) find that the turnover ratio of corporate bond is positively correlated with bond yield in the exchange market, but the relation reverses in the interbank market. However, there are still few studies that conduct comprehensive analysis about the determinants of cross-sectional corporate bond returns in China.In this paper, we study the relation between corporate bond characteristics and cross-sectional bond returns in China. We select main characteristics that are broadly used in the literature (Gebhardt et al., 2005; Lin et al., 2011; Dick-Neilsen et al., 2012; Huang and Zhan, 2021), including issuance size, time to maturity, bond age, numeric credit rating (a high number indicates high default risk), and Amihud illiquidity. We find that corporate bonds with small sizes, long maturities, old ages, poor credit ratings, and high Amihud illiquidity earn high monthly returns in the interbank market. Credit rating and Amihud illiquidity are the two predictors that significantly predict future bond returns in multivariate analysis. In terms of economic magnitudes, a one-standard-deviation increase in numeric credit rating (Amihud illiquidity) implies a 3.88 (2.77) basis points increase in corporate bond returns per month, or 46.56 (33.24) basis points per year. Bond size, time to maturity, and credit rating have similar return predictive patterns in the exchange market, but bond age and Amihud illiquidity predict returns in the opposite direction. These findings suggest that the relation between bond characteristics and corporate bond returns can be quite different in the two bond markets, implying market segmentation.We construct a credit risk factor and a liquidity risk factor based on credit rating and Amihud illiquidity to capture the common risks of corporate bonds—the credit risk and the liquidity risk. Then we build three factor models to explain the returns of corporate bond portfolios. The first model includes five common risk factors in Fama and French (1993): the stock market factor, the size factor, the value factor, the default spread, and the term spread (hereafter FF5). The second model consists of five factors from Chinese bond market, including the default spread, the term spread, the excess bond market return, the credit risk factor and the liquidity risk factor (hereafter Bond5). Our last model combines all eight factors together (hereafter Whole8).Finally, we use the Hansen–Jagannathan distance (hereafter HJ-distance) (Hansen and Jagannathan, 1997) to evaluate performances of the three factor models in pricing corporate bond portfolios. Using 14 univariate sorting corporate bond portfolios and the risk-free rate as our testing assets, the estimated HJ-distances in the interbank (exchange) market are 1.18 (0.62), 1.07 (0.51), and 0.90 (0.48) for the FF5, Bond5, and Whole8 models, respectively. Our findings indicate that the Whole8 model with the lowest model specification error outperforms the other two models, implying that characteristic-based bond factors can improve models' power to explain return variations in Chinese corporate bond portfolios. Our results also suggest that stock market factors can reduce model pricing errors for corporate bond portfolios.We contribute to literature in several aspects. First, to the best of our knowledge, this paper is one of the first studies to investigate the patterns of cross-sectional corporate bond returns in China's two bond markets. Recent research on China's corporate bond market includes studies by Fan and Zhang (2007) on the market segmentation of China's repo market, Chen et al. (2019) on the pricing of pledgeability, Geng and Pan (2019) on the premium on bonds issued by state-owned-enterprises (SOEs), Ding et al. (2022) on overpricing in the primary bond market, Liu et al. (2019) on investors' behaviors in the exchange bond market, Mo and Subrahmanyam (2020) on the liquidity of Chinese corporate bonds, and Flannery et al. (2022) on corporate borrowing costs. There is also a strand of literature on municipal corporate bonds and the implicit government guarantee (Liu et al., 2017; Ang et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2018). Our study complements the literature and draws attention to the characteristics and common factors that determine corporate bond returns in China.Second, we provide a formal evaluation of different factor models in pricing returns for corporate bond portfolios. In the literature, Fama and French (1993) show that both stock and bond factors are helpful in explaining corporate bond portfolio returns. Bai et al. (2019) indicate that downside risk, in combination with other bond market specific factors, best explains return variations in the U.S. corporate bond market. Chung et al. (2019) find that volatility risk, measured by changes in the VIX index, is priced into U.S. corporate bond returns. There is still a lack of empirical analysis on the magnitude of specification errors for various factor models. We contribute to the literature by formally estimating the HJ-distance of different factor models and evaluating model pricing errors for testing assets. According to our findings, the model that incorporates the excess corporate bond market return, a credit risk factor, a bond liquidity risk factor, and the five factors in Fama and French (1993) has the lowest model specification error.Finally, we provide evidence on whether return predictive patterns of bond characteristics can be different in China's two distinct and segmented bond markets. We find that the magnitude of the predictive power over returns for credit rating and Amihud illiquidity can be significantly different in the two markets. Our results are consistent with the notion that the two bond markets are segmented to some extent and indicate that distinct asset pricing models should be taken into account when investing in China's interbank and exchange bond markets.The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the institutional background and our sample. Section 3 presents the main results. Section 4 concludes the paper.2. Institutional background and data2.1. China's corporate bond marketBonds traded in China are categorized into three groups: government bonds, financial bonds, and corporate bonds (Amstad and He, 2019). Initially, government bonds (excluding municipal bonds) dominated the whole market, accounting for 89% of total bonds outstanding in 2008. However, with the development of China's economy, the corporate bond market has expanded rapidly. As shown in Panel A of Fig. 1, the value of corporate bonds outstanding rose from 1.26 trillion RMB in 2008 to 31.13 trillion RMB in 2022, and the proportion of corporate bonds in the overall market rose from 8% to 22% during this same period. Therefore, corporate bonds are gradually becoming an important component of investors' portfolios.

Corporate bonds outstanding in the interbank and the exchange markets.There are four major types of corporate bonds in China: commercial papers (CPs) with maturities of less than one year, medium-term notes (MTNs) with maturities between three and five years, enterprise bonds, and exchange-traded corporate bonds. Most corporate bonds in China are traded separately in one of the two markets. CPs and MTNs are only traded in the interbank market, whereas exchange-traded corporate bonds are traded in the exchange market. Since 2005, enterprise bonds have been permitted to be traded in both markets. As shown in Panel B of Fig. 1, the total amount of corporate bond outstanding in the interbank market is always larger than that in the exchange market, but the gap has narrowed in recent years. For instance, the difference in corporate bond outstanding between the interbank and the exchange markets was around 8 trillion RMB in 2015 and fell to 2.4 trillion RMB in 2022.Aside from bond types, the two bond markets differ in several other ways. Their trading mechanisms are different, as the interbank market is a quote-driven OTC market and the exchange market is an order-driven centralized market. The two markets have distinct supervisory institutions, namely the People's Bank of China in the interbank market and the China Security and Regulatory Commission in the exchange market. They also have different clearing and depository companies, resulting in a time-consuming procedure for cross-market security transfers. The other key difference is that the two markets are not accessible to all investors. The interbank market is only open to institutions, and commercial banks are the major participants. The exchange market is accessible to both institutional and retail investors.These differences between the interbank and exchange markets can cause severe segmentation and lead to different implications in terms of the determinants of cross-sectional corporate bond returns. For example, Liu et al. (2019) find that retail investors in the exchange market trade more high-yield bonds, leading to a positive correlation between turnover ratio and bond yield in the exchange market, but this relation reverses in the interbank market. Given these institutional facts, we conduct our empirical analysis separately for the interbank and the exchange markets and further investigate whether bond characteristics have distinct return predictive patterns in those two markets.2.2. Data2.2.1. SampleWe acquire corporate bond daily trading records and issuance information from Choice, one of the biggest financial data providers in China. In contrast to some data vendors, such as China Stock Market & Accounting Research Database, that only have access to data for either the interbank or the exchange market, Choice offers access to comprehensive data for both markets. Choice also provides detailed information on the characteristics of individual bonds and a long time series of daily trading data.We obtain data on all corporate bonds listed in the interbank and exchange markets that are issued by non-financial sectors from January 2010 to December 2022, and we apply several filters. First, we only retain bonds with fixed or zero-coupon rates. Then we exclude floating rate bonds because of the difficulty of tracking their cash flows, which leads to an inaccurate calculation of bond returns. We drop convertible and exchangeable bonds with option-like features because their return determinants are different from those traditional fixed income securities. We also remove bonds that are not traded in the public market, such as private placement notes, asset-backed securities, international institution bonds, and agency securities, such as railway bonds. Finally, we remove corporate bonds in default during our sample period.We calculate monthly corporate bond returns following the methodology in Bai et al. (2019). For each corporate bond, we have the close clean price, the accrued interest, and the trading amount in RMB. Choice also provides the date when the interest begins to accrue, the expiration date, the coupon rate, payment frequency, and the accrued interest calculation standard. The monthly bond return in month t for bond i is computed as in equation (1):(1)Ri,t=Pi,t+Ai,t+Ci,tPi,t−1+Ai,t−1,where Pi,t is the clean price for bond i in month t, Ai,t is the accrued interest, and Ci,t is the possible coupon payment. If there is no coupon in month t, Ci,t is zero.Unlike stocks, bonds typically have few trades in a month, particularly in the interbank market, meaning that Pi,t remains unchanged across consecutive days with no trades. Therefore, we only consider clean prices in our return calculation on days with trades at the end of the month t, where the end of the month refers to the last five trading days of each month. If there are multiple days with trades, we select the one closest to the end of the month. This method yields 40,588 bond-month observations. Furthermore, most enterprise bonds are issued with an early redemption clause. Once triggered, the clean prices of bonds plummet, resulting in abnormally low returns. To reduce the impact of extreme price fluctuations, we exclude 296 bond-month observations from the sample for which the absolute values of monthly bond returns are greater than the threshold.2The literature identifies several bond characteristics that are related to cross-sectional corporate bond returns (Gebhardt et al., 2005; Houweling et al., 2005; Jostova et al., 2013; Chordia et al., 2017). We select the most relevant and prevalent characteristics used in the literature. The first characteristic Size denotes the value of bonds outstanding in issuance. A bond with a high outstanding value suggests that the issuer has great financial demand and that the company is expected to have strong cash flows and be able to repay the huge debt. As a result, large-sized bonds may be associated with high bond prices and low expected returns. We use the logarithm of bond size, Lnsize, in our regression. The second characteristic, Maturity, is the time to maturity in years. Bonds with long maturities are exposed to more uncertainty than bonds with shorter maturities and are more sensitive to interest rate risks. Hence, they are acquired by investors with high expected returns. The third characteristic, Age, represents the bond duration in years since the time of issuance. According to Houweling et al. (2005), the age of a bond is a popular proxy for liquidity. An older bond is more likely to be held to maturity, seldom trades, and remains illiquid until it matures. Therefore, corporate bonds with old ages are expected to have high future returns.The fourth characteristic is the credit rating, which is used to assess the credit risk of corporate bonds. In China, long-term corporate bonds have nine rating grades (AAA, AA, A, BBB, BB, B, CCC, CC, C) and short-term corporate bond have six rating grades (A-1, A-2, A-3, B, C, D). To address the discrepancy in bond rating standards in our analysis, we employ the issue-level credit rating scales for CPs, which have nine ratings grades similar to long-term bonds. Following Bai et al. (2019), we obtain credit ratings for individual bonds from Choice at the end of each month and transform them to a numeric variable, Rating, to facilitate our analysis. For example, 1 represents the highest grade AAA, 2 denotes AA+, 3 denotes AA, 4 denotes AA-, and so on, until 19, which denotes the lowest grade, C. A high Rating value means that a bond has a high risk of default and hence is expected to pay high returns in the future. Most of Chinese corporate bonds have credit ratings above AA, with only a few having credit ratings below BBB+. As a result, an AA credit rating is considered as the lowest level for investment-grade corporate bonds by industries. As the distribution of credit ratings is skewed to the upside, we only retain observations with credit ratings above BBB, which exclude 12 observations from the sample.Prior literature shows that liquidity is related to corporate bond prices (Chen et al., 2007; Covitz and Downing, 2007; Edwards et al., 2007; Goldstein and Hotchkiss, 2020). We adopt the measure from Amihud (2002) to represent corporate bond illiquidity, as shown in equation (2),(2)Amihudi,t=1Ni∑d=1Ni|ri,d,t|Qi,d,t,where ri,d,t is the daily return for bond i on day d of month t, Qi,d,t is the trading amount in billions of RMB, and Ni is the total number of non-zero-volume trade days for bond i in month t. The Amihud variable is not missing if there is at least one non-zero-volume trading day in a month. A bond with a high Amihud value is expected to earn high future returns because of poor liquidity.There are several reasons why we think the Amihud illiquidity measure is an appropriate liquidity indicator in the Chinese corporate bond market. First, it is difficult to obtain high-frequency transaction information for corporate bonds in China, especially for those listed on the interbank market. Therefore, we cannot construct liquidity measures with transaction-level records. Second, many studies show that the Amihud measure is a valid liquidity proxy for corporate bonds in the U.S. market (Bao et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2011; Dick-Nielsen et al., 2012). Third, as Schestag et al. (2016) note, some liquidity measures, such as negative covariance (Roll, 1984) or the Gibbs measure (Hasbrouck, 2009), need more observations to obtain accurate estimations than the Amihud measure. To construct these metrics, we must use actively traded bonds, which may lead to a biased sample in our empirical analysis.Finally, bonds issued by SOEs and local government financial vehicles (LGFVs) have been attracting growing attention in recent years (Liu et al., 2017; Ang et al., 2018; Geng and Pan, 2019). Bonds issued by LGFVs, also known as municipal corporate bonds (MCBs), are viewed as avenues for local governments to finance infrastructure investment, particularly prior to the approval of municipal bond issuance in October 2014. Although it is widely believed that MCBs are implicitly guaranteed by the Chinese government, they still have potential default risks, which are influenced by the local economic situation and political risks (Ang et al., 2018). Therefore, to investigate the cross-sectional return patterns of SOE bonds and MCBs, we construct two dummy variables. The indicator variable SOE is equal to 1 if the corporate bonds are issued by SOEs, and zero otherwise. The indicator variable MCB is equal to 1 if the corporate bonds are issued by LGFVs, and zero otherwise.2.2.2. As mentioned in Section 2.2.1, we anticipate that corporate bonds with small sizes, long maturities, old ages, high numeric ratings, and large values of Amihud illiquidity earn high future returns. We predict bonds issued by SOEs and LGFVs to earn lower returns compared to their counterparties since they are thought to have lower default risks due to the assumed “implicit guarantee” offered by governments.3.1. Pricing of bond characteristicsWe first examine the relation between corporate bond characteristics and cross-sectional bond returns through the two-stage Fama–MacBeth regression (Fama and MacBeth, 1973), as in equation (3):(3)Ri,te=b0,t+b1,tLnsizei,t−1+b2,tMaturityi,t−1+b3,tRatingi,t−1+b4,tAgei,t−1+b5,tAmihudi,t−1+b6,tSOEi+b7,tMCBi+εi,t,where the monthly excess return Ri,te for bond i during the month t equals monthly return Reti,t minus the one-year deposit rate rft , a widely used proxy for China's risk-free rate (Liu, Stambaugh and Yuan, 2019). From the first-stage estimation, we obtain a time series of the cross-sectional coefficients {b0,tˆ,b1,tˆ,……,b7,tˆ}. In the second stage, we compute the time-series average of the coefficients as the final estimations. The standard errors are calculated using the Newey and West (1987) methodology with four lags, which is the optimal lag number under the Bayesian information criterion to adjust for potential time-series correlations in standard errors. To compare economic magnitudes across bond characteristics, we standardize all independent variables except for the two dummy indicators with a zero mean and unit standard deviation in each cross-sectional regression. We anticipate negative signs for {b1ˆ,b6ˆ,andb7ˆ} on Lnsize, SOE, and MCB respectively, but positive signs for the other coefficients.Panel A of Table 2 shows the estimation results for the interbank market. Columns 1 to 7 present the univariate regression results. The coefficients on Lnsize, Maturity, Age, Rating, and Amihud are −0.0247, 0.0562, 0.0084, 0.0546, and 0.0503, respectively, indicating that corporate bonds with small issuance sizes, long maturities, old ages, poor grade ratings, and large values of Amihud illiquidity earn high returns in the future. The corresponding t-statistics are −3.12, 2.06, 1.43, 4.59, and 4.08, respectively, meaning that all coefficients are significantly different from zero at least at the 95% confidence level except for the coefficient on Age. In terms of the economic magnitudes, a one-standard-deviation increase in bond maturity is related to a 5.62 basis points increase in corporate bond returns per month or 67.44 basis points per year, which is sizeable for corporate bond investment. The economic magnitudes of credit rating and Amihud illiquidity are slightly smaller than that of time-to-maturity. Because variables of bond characteristics are standardized with zero mean values, intercepts in these univariate regressions are the same. We do not find a significant difference in monthly returns between corporate bonds issued by SOEs and those issued by non-SOEs. However, MCBs earn higher monthly returns than non-MCB bonds, which contradicts the “implicit guarantee” hypothesis. Model comparisons with Hansen-Jagannathan distanceIn this section, we construct common risk factors based on bond characteristics and use Hansen-Jagannathan distance (Hansen and Jagannathan, 1997) to evaluate the performances of different factor models.First, following Fama and French (1993), we construct a five-factor model (hereafter FF5) using common risk factors in China's stock and bond markets. The model includes the stock market factor (MKTSTK), the size factor (SMB), the value factor (VMG), the default spread (DEF), and the term spread (TERM). We construct the three stock market factors following Liu, Stambaugh and Yuan (2019), which argue that their factor model explains cross-sectional stock returns well in China A-share market. The default spread is defined as the return difference between the ChinaBond 7–10 Year Credit Bond Index and the 7–10 Year Treasury Bond Index, which captures the aggregate default premium of corporate bonds.4 The term spread is defined as the return difference between the 7–10 Year and the 1–3 Year Treasury Bond Indices, which captures the term premium in China's bond market.Our second model (hereafter Bond5) is designed to capture common risks in China's bond markets and includes the default spread, the term spread, the excess interbank (exchange) bond market return (MKTIB(EX)), the credit risk factor (CREIB(EX)), and the liquidity risk factor (LIQIB(EX)). We use returns on a value-weighted corporate bond market portfolio to represent corporate bond market performances. To construct the liquidity and credit risk factors, we form bivariate portfolios by independently sorting corporate bonds into two groups based on whether their credit ratings are AAA or below and three tertiles based on their Amihud illiquidity measure. The liquidity risk factor, LIQ, is the value-weighted average return difference between the highest and the lowest-illiquidity portfolios across the credit rating portfolios. The credit risk factor, CRE, is the value-weighted average return difference between the portfolio containing bonds with credit ratings below AAA and the portfolio containing bonds with credit ratings equivalent to AAA across the liquidity portfolios. Our final model, Whole8, combines the eight factors from Chinese stock and bond markets.5We apply the HJ-distance to evaluate model performances of the three factor models. The HJ-distance is a metric of model specification errors and is extensively used in literature to assess asset pricing models in the stock market (Hodrick and Zhang, 2001; Zhang, 2006). The measure has several advantages in terms of model comparison. First, it is established on asset pricing theories and provides intuition on the form of the true stochastic discount factor (SDF). Second, it can be estimated using a traditional generalized method of moments (GMM) methodology, and we can simply perform statistical inference for hypothesis testing for model parameters. Finally, the metric can be easily explained as a maximum pricing error of expected return for a standardized portfolio.We briefly discuss the model setups, with more details provided in Hodrick and Zhang (2001). We use a linear combination of K common factors as a proxy for the true SDF mt. The linear SDF proxy yt is shown in equation (5):(5)yt=b′Ft=b0+b1′ft,where ft is the k×1 factor vector and b1 is the k×1 coefficient vector. Then, the Hansen–Jagannathan metric is the distance between the SDF proxy y and the true SDF m, as in equation (6):(6)δ=minmtE[(yt−mt)2]1/2s.t.E(Rtmt)=p.where Rt is the N×1 return vector and p is the price vector for N assets. If Rt is the (gross) excess return, then the values in the price vector equal (one) zero.The HJ-distance δ and parameters b can be estimated through GMM with the weighted matrix of E(RtRt′)−1. With an additional assumption that E(yt)=E(mt)=Rf−1, where Rf is the gross return of a risk-free asset, the HJ-distance can be explained by the maximum pricing error of the expected return for a specific portfolio, which equals Rfδ multiplied by a standard deviation.6 We use the standard deviation of corporate bond returns, which is around 3.45% per year, to calculate the maximum pricing error. Jagannathan and Wang (1996) induct the distribution of the HJ-distance, and we report the corresponding p-value under the null hypothesis that the HJ-distance equals zero. We also calculate p-values under the Wald test that the estimated parameters are jointly zero and the J test that the pricing errors of the sample moment conditions are jointly different from zero under the optimal GMM estimation. The maximum pricing error is around 4% per year. The p-value for the Wald test indicates that the coefficients of all factors jointly differ from zero at the 99% confidence level, meaning that some factors have pricing power for these tested assets. Finally, the p-value for the J test is 0.18, suggesting that under the optimal GMM estimations, the pricing errors of the sample moment conditions are jointly zero.
