IntroductionToday, in some countries, cryptocurrency is recognized as an instrument of payment and may be accepted as payment (Sonksen, 2021). Institutions recognize cryptocurrency as property, thereby establishing in its relation the rights of possession, use and disposal. The FATF (Financial Action Task Force on Money Laundering) recommendations require national financial intelligence agencies to establish rules related to the regulation of cryptocurrencies, including identification during transactions and the study of their origin (Financial Action Task Force, 2021; OECD, 2020). Closely related to this is the problem of evaluating borrowers who take loans secured by cryptocurrencies. An incorrect assessment of the borrower can make, among other things, a bona fide lender a party to an illegal transaction. Here, financial and credit institutions’ goals are closely linked to the more general problem of identifying participants in networks using cryptocurrencies that are engaged in suspicious activity. Criminal activities here are usually associated with the processes of depositing and withdrawing money into cryptocurrency. Among the schemes for converting cryptocurrencies into fiat money, it is worth mentioning the classic currency exchange; purchase of goods for cryptocurrency with subsequent sale for fiat money; obtaining a loan in fiat money secured by cryptocurrency for subsequent fraud or credit default. As for the latter, along with credit scoring, the development of classification models capable of identifying ML/TF agents is included in the general agenda of essential tasks for researching the Bitcoin network.In recent years, accompanied by a rise in the price of bitcoin, loans secured by cryptocurrency have become widespread, see (Wang et al., 2019). Evidence of this is the increase in the number of requests for such loans, demonstrating a specific correlation with the bitcoin rate (according to Google Trends (e), see Fig. 1, Table 1).

By searching for the queries indicated in the legend of the chart (see Fig. 1), one will be able to discover links to services for providing crypto-backed loans.Peer-to-peer (P2P) lending has become a natural form of lending secured by cryptocurrency (Financial Action Task Force, 2021). P2P lending allows network members to receive loans and issue loans without intermediaries, including financial institutions. In a decentralized P2P lending system using distributed ledger technology, lending can be carried out between any interested users without the participation of any financial institution and provide better conditions for borrowers and lenders.Using smart contracts supported by a decentralized network generally speeds up the lending process, reduces overhead costs, and provides certain guarantees regarding security based on risk assessment. At the same time, the specific features of digital immersion raise a problem of scoring borrowers. In the extreme digital version, they can only be represented by their digital profile (or, in general, by a wallet, as it happens in permissionless blockchain networks).To solve the scoring problem, it is required to study and evaluate the digital version of the borrower's credit history. Moreover, this should be done in an environment where anonymity and de-individualization are considered one of the main advantages and are provided by network protocols. In its formulation, this problem is certainly related to the problem of traditional scoring, however, the characteristics of cryptocurrencies lead to the emergence of a number of problems that are specific to this type of collateralized lending. For example, how to isolate from the client's transactions those that can be considered as receiving and returning a loan to cryptocurrency or depositing/returning cryptocurrency to a deposit as collateral for a loan for fiat money. Without additional data, it is impossible to understand whether the client has stopped paying due to default or due to the exhaustion of scheduled payments. Solving these problems allows for reducing the risks associated with lending secured by cryptocurrency.To receive relatively small amounts in P2P lending, collateral is usually not required. For larger amounts, a deposit may be required. In P2P lending, even in the absence of collateral, the average interest rate is usually lower than the national average (for the BRICS countries) but with shorter repayment periods than traditional lending. Using the blockchain platform, the borrower can enter information about himself (about the project), upload verifiable documents, and request the loan amount. Platforms that provide P2P lending use multiple sources of verification to assign this request a quality score (a sort of credit score). Lenders offer interest and repayment rates. Once the requested amount is collected and accepted, the smart contract comes into play. The minimum interest rate of platforms that issue loans secured by cryptocurrency ranges from 1% to 6%. Maximum interest rates for lenders vary from 9.6% to 21.49%, maximum LTV (Loan-to-Value, a ratio to determine the amount of cryptocurrency, valued in USD or EUR, required depositing to a lender) for platforms varies from 50% to 83% see (https://linen.app/interest-rates, accessed 2 May 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). Loan terms, as a rule, are about six months, but loans with shorter (up to 1 month) and longer (up to three years) terms are also possible.Official crypto lending platforms that operate in the legal field, including those with a European virtual currency provider license, have the following service scheme in general terms (https://help.coinloan.io/en/articles/6572562-how-to-get-a-loan-instruction, accessed 2 May 2023; https://nexo.com/borrow, accessed 2 May 2023.). First of all, the client must register. Then she presents an application for a loan. The platform, in response, indicates the terms of the loan, particularly the amount of collateral in cryptocurrency, and invites the client to provide proof of ownership of the cryptocurrency in the required amount. After presenting the evidence, the platform evaluates the collateral. In addition to liquidity and market value, this assessment includes an examination of involvement in prohibited activities (fraud, money laundering, terrorism financing). Based on the verification which is recommended by FATF regarding to virtual assets operators and providers (Updated, 2021), a decision is made to grant a loan. In case of a positive decision, the issuance of a loan and the acceptance of collateral are formalized by an appropriate smart contract. The platform assumes the risks of the lender, covering the lender’s losses if they occur. Note that some P2P lending platforms provide collector users operating in the digital space.To mitigate risk, lenders need to have more detailed information about the borrower as a digital system user than is provided in their profile. Such information can be obtained from the analysis of the borrower's transactions. This is in a sense a replacement for a credit history. Relevant databases contain sufficiently complete information about transactions. The problem is how to identify a "suspicious" borrower by transactions. If cryptocurrency is used as collateral then the same problem can arise for conventional lending institutions as well.This paper describes the algorithm for detecting the illegal activities of a digital client in the blockchain network. The research is in line with the development of suspicious patterns of behavior, formed and analyzed for over a decade. In particular, the problem of identifying suspicious clients in the Bitcoin network is considered. The basics for the formation of templates were created by special services (Federal Bureau of Investigation, 2012), however, the use of the methods proposed by them by the creators of P2P lending platforms does not provide sufficient efficiency, see (Berg, 2020). The complexity of analyzing transactions in the Bitcoin network is objective and due to the lack of linking transactions to the owner of the address. Thus, it is required that the "suspicious" label should be received not by network clients but by addresses (wallets). The paper proposes an approach based on data mining. An experimental study of various data analysis methods showed that gradient boosting gives the best result. Quality comparable to and better than in the most advanced studies is achieved here using fewer features and less depth of data aggregation, indicating the results’ reliability, cf. This dataset provides information on more than 8,000 Bitcoin addresses. More than 9% of these addresses are related to money laundering using mixing services. Cryptocurrencies and illegal financial activitiesThe rise of the Bitcoin cryptocurrency marked a new era of illegal transactions. Cryptocurrency itself provides some level of anonymity. Cryptocurrency allows its users to create an unlimited number of wallets with pseudonymous addresses, making it challenging to identify a real user. This is used by criminals to carry out illegal operations related to money laundering, terrorist financing, drug trafficking, etc. At the same time, Bitcoin stores and makes public information about all transactions. The stock of transactions provides opportunities for detecting suspicious behavior using data mining. Some datasets, including the one published by Elliptic (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ellipticco/elliptic-data-set, accessed 2 May 2023.), contain the markup of addresses in the Bitcoin network and enables researchers to build machine learning models based on provided transaction feature or on self-built transaction features which can be acquired using public databases. This allows developing and analyzing methods of supervised learning to reveal suspicious behavior.Cryptocurrency is "electronic cash" based on electronic records in an open decentralized distributed ledger. On the one hand, cryptographic algorithms protect the information from unauthorized correction. On the other hand, these algorithms ensure information availability to the participant and owners of wallets (addresses). The most popular cryptocurrency Bitcoin was developed and presented in 2008 (Nakamoto, 2008). The purpose of creating bitcoin was to reduce costs by eliminating intermediaries in transactions. Transactions are confirmed using consensus algorithms providing the consent of the user community to add a block of transactions to the ledger.Bitcoin is often used for suspicious or criminal activities such as fraud, extortion, black market trading, money laundering, and terrorist financing. Basically, bitcoin guarantees the anonymity of transactions. Complete anonymization requires creating a new address for each new transaction and not making transactions with more than one sender. However, users, including cybercriminals, often ignore these rules. Thus, it becomes possible to link groups of addresses and identify suspicious addresses and transactions using big data analysis (Zheng et al., 2020).A distributed ledger can be represented as a directed graph. The vertices of this graph are associated with wallet addresses and the edges with transactions. Additional information is specified for the edges: the amount of the transaction, the timestamp, the miner's commission, etc. The node of this graph can be characterized by a set of data associated with outgoing and incoming edges.FATF regularly updates recommendations on establishing a legal regime for customer identification in transactions with cryptocurrencies, in particular, requiring compliance with the so-called “Know Your Customer” (KYC) principle (Alkadri, 2018). However, the proposed legal liability does not seem to be fully effective due to the extraterritorial principle of Bitcoin. In this regard, it becomes relevant to study the machine learning methods to classify suspicious bitcoin addresses based on the characteristics of transactions.Researchers from different countries developed approaches to identifying suspicious addresses. Network anomaly detection methods have found application in many areas: credit card fraud detection (Belle and Mitrovic, 2019), cybersecurity (Bö et al., 2018), social networks (Akhtar and Ahamad, 2021), etc. According to the Global Economic Crime Survey 2016 (Global Economic Crime Survey, 2016), worldwide money laundering operations are estimated at about 1–2 trillion US dollars annually. The same study states that only 50% of money laundering or terrorist financing cases were detected using warning systems. In (Marcin, 2015), it is argued that banks’ fraud detection algorithms (anti-fraud models) are not applicable to the specifics of cryptocurrencies. However, methods allowing the deanonymization of Bitcoin users are described in (ShenTu and Yu, 2015). Deanonymization makes it possible to request information that directly characterizes a person. Accordingly, the models are applicable using predictors based on conventional socio-demographic data.The analysis of network connections to detect money laundering is one of the classic methods used in law enforcement intelligence (see (Bolton and Hand, 2004)). The distribution of connections between nodes and connectivity properties of subgraphs characterize communities. Thus, if the label "illegal" is assigned to several community members, then this label can be generalized (under certain assumptions) to all participants.Until recently, law enforcement has had difficulties detecting suspicious activity, identifying real users, and obtaining transaction records (Meiklejohn et al., 2013). That is why Bitcoin network has remained attractive to cybercriminals. Today, both law enforcement agencies and organizations that need to track suspicious activity are developing and implementing methodologies for identifying suspicious behaviors based on characteristics tracked in the public Bitcoin network (see, for example, (Federal Bureau of Investigation, 2012), which describes the US FBI approach).To date, the industry of researching Bitcoin, Ethereum and other blockchains has been formed. Among the leader services are Chainalysis, Crystal, Elliptic, BlockSci (see (Balaskas and Franqueira, 2018) for detailed analysis.) These tools are currently used by law enforcement because they provide links between blockchain address and real-life entities using private databases containing millions of records and proprietary information. In particular, Chainalysis provides an API for embedding in real processes of wallet verification and KYC/KYT compliance (https://www.chainalysis.com/solutions/compliance/, accessed 2 May 2023.).3. Collecting labels and transaction dataThe addresses in the Bitcoin network in their "raw" form are not labeled according to their belonging to a certain category. This is the main problem with the use of machine learning classification methods. There is no explicit reference to the owner of an address in the Bitcoin blockchain. Thus, the researcher or a third party must assign address labels identifying "honest" or "suspicious" addresses based on web search, own experience or special methods (Wang et al., 2020).Some services provide public databases of tagged addresses.3.1. WalletExplorerThis service uses an automatic search on the Internet to identify addresses and connect them to wallets. Some wallets are tagged with the services they were found on. There are labels for gambling, cryptocurrency exchanges, dark web stores, fraudulent Ponzi scheme sites, and mixing services.3.2. BitcoinAbuseThis public database provides reports of malicious behavior related to specific wallets. Most often, users tag addresses as spam, less often tags are associated with extortion, fraud, and other crimes. Although information from one particular user is difficult to verify, it is possible to filter out addresses where transactions were made and/or multiple complaints were made.These services have an API (Application Programming Interface) that allows collection of statistical information about "honest" and "suspicious" agents automatically.Information about transaction addresses can be collected in several ways. The first way is to download the entire blockchain and collect information about all transactions using a parser. This method requires significant disk space and parsing skills, but once prepared, it allows one to quickly collect all the information, including transaction data, regarding a particular address. The second way is to use external data providers (like BitAps.com, Blockchain.info), and request information using an API (Application Programming Interface). However, API providers typically impose limits on the number of requests per unit of time and the number of transactions per request because the number of transactions per address can be significant.4. Comparing studies based on Elliptic DatasetThe Elliptic Dataset (Michalski et al., 2020c) contains information on 203,769 transactions. Elliptic, a private company specializing in protecting cryptocurrency ecosystems from criminal activities, prepared this data set. About 2% of transactions are flagged as "suspicious". There are reasons to believe these transactions are related to suspicious groups or individuals such as scammers, malware, terrorist organizations, ransomware, and Ponzi schemes. About 21% of the total number of transactions belongs to legal users such as exchanges, wallet providers, miners, legal services, etc. The rest of the transactions are unlabeled.For each transaction, the time of its inclusion in the block and confirmation by the Bitcoin network is indicated. The data set under consideration uses 49 time intervals, evenly distributed over an interval of about two weeks (specific dates are not indicated). Each time interval is matched to a list of related transactions. The list contains transactions that appeared on the blockchain in less than 3 h. It is worth noting that there are no connections relating transactions assigned to different time steps.For each address, the data set contains 166 features based on information about the address itself. It should be noted that the set contains addresses and features in legend form. Before publication, the predictors were subject to obfuscation: they were standardized (mean 0, standard deviation 1), and their names were hidden. According to (Michalski et al., 2020b), the first group of 94 predictors represents local information about the transaction, including the time step, the number of inputs and outputs, the miner's fee, the input amount, and some aggregated values (the average amount and the number of inputs and outputs of the transaction). The second group of 72 predictors contains aggregated information about "neighboring" transactions (one step forward/backward from the main address). Aggregation was carried out by calculating the minimum, maximum and standard deviation of features similar to those of the first group. While the data itself can be used to build classifiers, predictor obfuscation leaves open the question of how the data is actually used to identify suspicious addresses in a real environment.This article explores the binary classification of addresses.In general, the problem can be formulated as follows. There is a set of objects X (potentially infinite) and a set of labels Y={y1,...,ys}. Let S⊂X×Y be a finite training sample. We suppose that S is a functional relation on X. An algorithm must be built such that it calculates a function a:X→Y and S is a subset of the graph of an up to a negligibly small number of points.In (Feldman et al., 2021), a binary classification (with s=2) is carried out to predict illegal transactions. Logit regression, random forest, multilayer perceptron and convolutional neural network are the main methods in (Lorenz et al., 2020). The data is divided into training and test subsets with a ratio of 70:30. Random forest provides the best result (Table 2).Table 2. Metrics for binary classification models.

Building a classifier for suspicious addressesThis study used a labeled set of Bitcoin addresses published on the Harvard website. Dataverse is an open repository at Harvard University (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ellipticco/elliptic-data-set, accessed 2 May 2023.). The set contains information about blocks with a height from 520890 (timestamp 2018-05-02 16:29) to 520910 (2018-05-02 20:06). By collecting information from WalletExplorer, public forums, websites of specific services, 8808 addresses were marked up for the following types of addresses (the number of addresses is indicated in brackets):miners (4030) – users who are engaged in the confirmation of transaction blocks;mining pools (89) – associations of miners;exchangers (1666) – platforms that provide the opportunity to buy or sell Bitcoin in exchange for fiat money or another cryptocurrency;mixing services (800) – platforms that provide services to hide transactions, usually related to illegal trade;platforms for accepting bets and gambling (911);other services (1312) – sites that accept Bitcoin as payment.Two services (blockchain explorers) were used to collect information about transactions associated with these addresses: BitAps.com, Blockchain.Info. First, BitAps.com downloaded the hashes of the last 250 transactions for each address. Then, detailed information about senders, recipients, amounts, and other data was received from the Blockchain.Info website for each transaction uploaded at the previous step. A total of 578809 transactions were collected.The information about address transactions makes it possible to build predictors and train address classification models.Data transformation is carried out at two levels:Each transaction is transformed into a one-dimensional set of features of a fixed length;For each address, aggregated information is collected on transactions in which the address took part as a sender or recipient, and derived predictors are calculated.For every predictor, the empirical probability density function was examined regarding to each wallet type. Let us indicate some typical features of the distributions which can be used as markers of suspicion.For mixing services, an increased commission is typical, and for mining pools, a reduced one is. Mining pools make multiple coinbase transactions, so the density of the average number of senders per address is shifted strongly to the left. In contrast, currency exchangers have the highest average number of senders. A sharp peak in the distribution of the average number of outgoing addresses in transactions for mixing services indicates a strong concentration of the number of recipient addresses. The median is two addresses. It can be seen that for exchangers addresses, the number of both senders and recipients shifts to the right. Incoming amounts for addresses owned by mixing services are highly concentrated: the median value is 6.69 BTC. This confirms the assumption that frequent repetition of transactions with the same amount is a marker of suspicion.For further modeling,we used the freely distributed scikit-learn machine learning package for Python 3, and for gradient boosting we used Microsoft LightGBM package. The modeling process included preparation of a dataset using the data aggregation method for the last 250 transactions by addresses; splitting the sample into training and test with a 80:20 ratio; training classifier models; quality measuring on a test sample. Logistic regressionThe algorithm computes a weighted sum of the input features, including a bias term, and then applies a logistic function to the result. The logistic function maps any input value to a value between 0 and 1, which can be interpreted as a probability (Hosmer et al., 2013).5.2. Decision treeThe model consists of nodes and branches, where each node represents a feature and each branch represents a possible value of that feature. The root node represents the most important feature, and the algorithm selects the feature that provides the best split of the data at each node, which can be found by maximizing some function like GINI Gain or Information Gain (Tangirala, 2020).5.3. Random forestAn ensemble learning algorithm that combines multiple decision trees to improve the accuracy and robustness of the model. The algorithm creates a group of decision trees, where each tree is trained on a random subset of the input features and a random subset of the input data. During training, the random forest algorithm builds multiple decision trees using a technique called bagging, which involves randomly sampling the input data with replacement. Each decision tree is trained on a different subset of the data, and the final prediction is made by aggregating the predictions of all the trees (Oshiro et al., 2012).5.4. Gradient boostingA machine learning algorithm that combines multiple weak learners – decision trees – to create a strong model. The algorithm works by sequentially adding new trees to the ensemble, where each new model is trained to correct the residual errors of the previous models, with the goal of minimizing the overall error of the ensemble (Benté et al., 2021). In our research, we have used the LightGBM modification of the algorithm (Ke et al., 2017).The main selected hyperparameters of the algorithms are presented in Table 4.Table 4. Hyperparameters.

Application concept for analyzing blockchain transactionsThe developed classifier model can be operated through a web application. As part of this work, we propose a prototype of such an application developed on the Flask framework. Its main feature is a web form for user requests for specific Bitcoin addresses. By querying the address, the user receives a prediction of the model, and a report on the last 250 transactions of the subject address and on other addresses with which this address has made transactions.We have used an implementation pattern called Model-as-Dependency. This is a way to embed our trained model into the application as part of it: the model is supplied as a separate file, and the application itself has an internal logical structure that accesses the file to pass features and call the prediction method.The initial version uses a basic structure with a local software server provided by the Flask package. ConclusionThe problem of detecting suspicious activity in the Bitcoin network can be solved with a reasonably high accuracy using machine learning methods. The basic information structure is a directed graph where nodes (addresses) are related by edges (transactions). The random forest and gradient boosting methods show the best results in identifying suspicious addresses in the Bitcoin network.
