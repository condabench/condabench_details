**Cricket** is a [bat\-and\-ball game](/wiki/Bat-and-ball_games "Bat-and-ball games") played between two [teams](/wiki/Sports_team "Sports team") of eleven players on a [field](/wiki/Cricket_field "Cricket field"), at the centre of which is a 22\-yard (20\-metre; 66\-foot) [pitch](/wiki/Cricket_pitch "Cricket pitch") with a [wicket](/wiki/Wicket "Wicket") at each end, each comprising two [bails](/wiki/Bail_(cricket) "Bail (cricket)") (small sticks) balanced on three [stumps](/wiki/Stump_(cricket) "Stump (cricket)"). Two players from the [batting](/wiki/Batting_(cricket) "Batting (cricket)") team, the striker and nonstriker, stand in front of either wicket holding [bats](/wiki/Cricket_bat "Cricket bat"), while one player from the [fielding](/wiki/Fielding_(cricket) "Fielding (cricket)") team, the bowler, [bowls](/wiki/Bowling_(cricket) "Bowling (cricket)") the [ball](/wiki/Cricket_ball "Cricket ball") toward the striker's wicket from the opposite end of the pitch. The striker's goal is to hit the bowled ball with the bat and then switch places with the nonstriker, with the batting team scoring one [run](/wiki/Run_(cricket) "Run (cricket)") for each of these exchanges. Runs are also scored when the ball reaches the [boundary](/wiki/Boundary_(cricket) "Boundary (cricket)") of the field or when the ball is bowled [illegally](/wiki/Illegal_delivery_(cricket) "Illegal delivery (cricket)").


The fielding team tries to prevent runs from being scored by [dismissing](/wiki/Dismissal_(cricket) "Dismissal (cricket)") batters (so they are "out"). Means of dismissal include being [bowled](/wiki/Bowled "Bowled"), when the ball hits the striker's wicket and dislodges the bails, and by the fielding side either [catching](/wiki/Caught "Caught") the ball after it is hit by the bat but before it hits the ground, or hitting a wicket with the ball [before](/wiki/Run_out "Run out") a batter can cross the [crease line](/wiki/Crease_(cricket) "Crease (cricket)") in front of the wicket. When ten batters have been dismissed, the [innings](/wiki/Innings "Innings") (playing phase) ends and the teams swap roles. [Forms of cricket](/wiki/Forms_of_cricket "Forms of cricket") range from traditional [Test matches](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") played over five days to the newer [Twenty20](/wiki/Twenty20 "Twenty20") format (also known as *T20*), in which each team bats for a single innings of 20 [overs](/wiki/Over_(cricket) "Over (cricket)") (each "over" being a set of 6 fair opportunities for the batting team to score) and the game generally lasts three to four hours.


Traditionally, cricketers play in all\-white [kit](/wiki/Cricket_clothing_and_equipment "Cricket clothing and equipment"), but in [limited overs cricket](/wiki/Limited_overs_cricket "Limited overs cricket"), they wear club or team colours. In addition to the basic kit, some players wear protective gear to prevent injury caused by the ball, which is a hard, solid spheroid made of compressed [leather](/wiki/Leather "Leather") with a slightly raised sewn seam enclosing a [cork](/wiki/Cork_(material) "Cork (material)") core layered with tightly wound string.


The earliest known definite reference to cricket is to it being played in [South East England](/wiki/South_East_England "South East England") in the mid\-16th century. It spread globally with the expansion of the [British Empire](/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire"), with the first international matches in the second half of the 19th century. The game's governing body is the [International Cricket Council](/wiki/International_Cricket_Council "International Cricket Council") (ICC), which has over 100 members, twelve of which are [full members](/wiki/List_of_International_Cricket_Council_members "List of International Cricket Council members") who play Test matches. The game's rules, the *[Laws of Cricket](/wiki/Laws_of_Cricket "Laws of Cricket")*, are maintained by [Marylebone Cricket Club](/wiki/Marylebone_Cricket_Club "Marylebone Cricket Club") (MCC) in [London](/wiki/London "London"). The sport is followed primarily in [South Asia](/wiki/South_Asia "South Asia"), [Australia](/wiki/Australia "Australia"), [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand "New Zealand"), the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom"), [Southern Africa](/wiki/Southern_Africa "Southern Africa"), and the [West Indies](/wiki/West_Indies "West Indies").[\[2]](#cite_note-:0-2)


[Women's cricket](/wiki/Women%27s_cricket "Women's cricket"), which is organised and played separately, has also achieved international standard.


The most successful side playing [international cricket](/wiki/International_cricket "International cricket") is [Australia](/wiki/Australia_national_cricket_team "Australia national cricket team"), which has won eight [One Day International](/wiki/One_Day_International "One Day International") trophies, including six [World Cups](/wiki/Cricket_World_Cup "Cricket World Cup"), more than any other country, and has been the [top\-rated Test side](/wiki/ICC_Test_Championship "ICC Test Championship") more than any other country.\[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]




History
-------


Main article: [History of cricket](/wiki/History_of_cricket "History of cricket")
### Origins


Main article: [History of cricket to 1725](/wiki/History_of_cricket_to_1725 "History of cricket to 1725")


A medieval "club ball" game involving an underarm bowl towards a batter. Ball catchers are shown positioning themselves to catch a ball. Detail from the *[Canticles of Holy Mary](/wiki/Cantigas_de_Santa_Maria "Cantigas de Santa Maria")*, 13th century.


Cricket is one of many games in the "club ball" sphere that involve hitting a ball with a hand\-held implement. Others include [baseball](/wiki/Baseball "Baseball") (which shares many [similarities](/wiki/Comparison_of_baseball_and_cricket "Comparison of baseball and cricket") with cricket, both belonging in the more specific [bat\-and\-ball games](/wiki/Bat-and-ball_games "Bat-and-ball games") category[\[3]](#cite_note-3)), [golf](/wiki/Golf "Golf"), [hockey](/wiki/Hockey "Hockey"), [tennis](/wiki/Tennis "Tennis"), [squash](/wiki/Squash_(sport) "Squash (sport)"), [badminton](/wiki/Badminton "Badminton") and [table tennis](/wiki/Table_tennis "Table tennis").[\[4]](#cite_note-JM17-4) In cricket's case, a key difference is the existence of a solid target structure, the wicket (originally, it is thought, a "[wicket gate](/wiki/Wicket_gate "Wicket gate")" through which sheep were herded), that the batter must defend.[\[5]](#cite_note-BWC1-5) The cricket historian [Harry Altham](/wiki/Harry_Altham "Harry Altham") identified three "groups" of "club ball" games: the "hockey group", in which the ball is driven to and from between two targets (the goals); the "golf group", in which the ball is driven towards an undefended target (the hole); and the "cricket group", in which "the ball is aimed at a mark (the wicket) and driven away from it".[\[6]](#cite_note-HSA19-6)


It is generally believed that cricket originated as a [children's game](/wiki/Children%27s_game "Children's game") in the south\-eastern counties of England, sometime during the [medieval period](/wiki/England_in_the_Middle_Ages "England in the Middle Ages").[\[5]](#cite_note-BWC1-5) Although there are claims for prior dates, the earliest definite reference to cricket being played comes from evidence given at a court case in [Guildford](/wiki/Guildford "Guildford") in January 1597 ([Old Style](/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates "Old Style and New Style dates"), equating to January 1598 in the modern calendar). The case concerned ownership of a certain plot of land, and the court heard the testimony of a 59\-year\-old [coroner](/wiki/Coroner "Coroner"), [John Derrick](/wiki/John_Derrick_(coroner) "John Derrick (coroner)"), who gave witness that:[\[7]](#cite_note-HSA21-7)[\[8]](#cite_note-DU3-8)[\[9]](#cite_note-JM19-9)




> Being a scholler in the [ffree schoole of Guldeford](/wiki/Royal_Grammar_School,_Guildford "Royal Grammar School, Guildford") hee and diverse of his fellows did runne and play there at creckett and other plaies.


Given Derrick's age, it was about half a century earlier when he was at school, and so it is certain that cricket was being played c. 1550 by boys in [Surrey](/wiki/Surrey "Surrey").[\[9]](#cite_note-JM19-9) The view that it was originally a children's game is reinforced by [Randle Cotgrave](/wiki/Randle_Cotgrave "Randle Cotgrave")'s 1611 English\-[French](/wiki/French_language "French language") dictionary in which he defined the noun "*crosse*" as "the crooked staff wherewith boys play at cricket", and the verb form "*crosser*" as "to play at cricket".[\[10]](#cite_note-HSA22-10)[\[11]](#cite_note-JM31-11)


One possible source for the sport's name is the [Old English](/wiki/Old_English_language "Old English language") word "*cryce*" (or "*cricc*") meaning a crutch or staff. In [Samuel Johnson](/wiki/Samuel_Johnson "Samuel Johnson")'s *Dictionary*, he derived cricket from "*cryce*, Saxon, a stick".[\[7]](#cite_note-HSA21-7) In [Old French](/wiki/Old_French "Old French"), the word "*criquet*" seems to have meant a kind of club or stick.[\[12]](#cite_note-DB3-12) Given the strong medieval trade connections between south\-east England and the [County of Flanders](/wiki/County_of_Flanders "County of Flanders") when the latter belonged to the [Duchy of Burgundy](/wiki/Duchy_of_Burgundy "Duchy of Burgundy"), the name may have been derived from the [Middle Dutch](/wiki/Middle_Dutch "Middle Dutch") (in use in Flanders at the time) "*krick*"(*\-e*), meaning a stick (crook).[\[12]](#cite_note-DB3-12) Another possible source is the Middle Dutch word "*krickstoel*", meaning a long low stool used for kneeling in church that resembled the long low [wicket](/wiki/Wicket "Wicket") with two [stumps](/wiki/Stump_(cricket) "Stump (cricket)") used in early cricket.[\[13]](#cite_note-13) According to Heiner Gillmeister, a European language expert of [Bonn University](/wiki/Bonn_University "Bonn University"), "cricket" derives from the Middle Dutch phrase for hockey, "*met de (krik ket)sen*" ("with the stick chase").[\[14]](#cite_note-17CGC-14) Gillmeister has suggested that not only the name but also the sport itself may be of Flemish origin.[\[14]](#cite_note-17CGC-14)



### Growth of amateur and professional cricket in England




Evolution of the cricket bat. The original "[hockey stick](/wiki/Hockey_stick "Hockey stick")" (left) evolved into the straight bat from c. 1760, when [pitched delivery bowling](/wiki/Pitched_delivery_bowling "Pitched delivery bowling") began.


Although the main object of the game has always been to score the most [runs](/wiki/Run_(cricket) "Run (cricket)"), the early form of cricket differed from the modern game in certain key technical aspects; the North American variant of cricket known as [wicket](/wiki/Wicket_(sport) "Wicket (sport)") retained many of these aspects.[\[15]](#cite_note-15) The [ball](/wiki/Cricket_ball "Cricket ball") was [bowled underarm](/wiki/Underarm_bowling "Underarm bowling") by the [bowler](/wiki/Bowling_(cricket) "Bowling (cricket)") and along the ground towards a [batter](/wiki/Batting_(cricket) "Batting (cricket)") armed with a [bat](/wiki/Cricket_bat "Cricket bat") that in shape resembled a [hockey stick](/wiki/Hockey_stick "Hockey stick"); the batter defended a low, two\-stump [wicket](/wiki/Wicket "Wicket"); and runs were called notches because the [scorers](/wiki/Scoring_(cricket) "Scoring (cricket)") recorded them by notching tally sticks.[\[16]](#cite_note-DB9-16)[\[17]](#cite_note-BWC2-17)[\[18]](#cite_note-JM21-18)


In 1611, the year [Cotgrave](/wiki/Randle_Cotgrave "Randle Cotgrave")'s dictionary was published, [ecclesiastical court](/wiki/Ecclesiastical_court "Ecclesiastical court") records at [Sidlesham](/wiki/Sidlesham "Sidlesham") in [Sussex](/wiki/Sussex "Sussex") state that two parishioners, Bartholomew Wyatt and Richard Latter, failed to attend church on [Easter Sunday](/wiki/Easter_Sunday "Easter Sunday") because they were playing cricket. They were fined 12[d](/wiki/Penny_(British_pre-decimal_coin) "Penny (British pre-decimal coin)") each and ordered to do [penance](/wiki/Penance "Penance").[\[19]](#cite_note-19) This is the earliest mention of adult participation in cricket and it was around the same time that the earliest known organised inter\-parish or [village](/wiki/Village_cricket "Village cricket") match was played, at [Chevening, Kent](/wiki/Chevening,_Kent "Chevening, Kent").[\[7]](#cite_note-HSA21-7)[\[20]](#cite_note-DU4-20) In 1624, a player called [Jasper Vinall](/wiki/Jasper_Vinall "Jasper Vinall") died after he was accidentally struck on the head during a match between two parish teams in Sussex.[\[21]](#cite_note-21)


Cricket remained a low\-key local pursuit for much of the 17th century.[\[11]](#cite_note-JM31-11) It is known, through numerous references found in the records of ecclesiastical court cases, to have been proscribed at times by the [Puritans](/wiki/Puritans "Puritans") before and during the [Commonwealth](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_England "Commonwealth of England").[\[22]](#cite_note-22)[\[23]](#cite_note-DU11-23) The problem was nearly always the issue of Sunday play, as the Puritans considered cricket to be "profane" if played on the [Sabbath](/wiki/Sabbath "Sabbath"), especially if large crowds or [gambling](/wiki/Sport_gambling "Sport gambling") were involved.[\[24]](#cite_note-DB7-24)[\[25]](#cite_note-JM23-25)


According to the social historian [Derek Birley](/wiki/Derek_Birley "Derek Birley"), there was a "great upsurge of sport after the [Restoration](/wiki/English_Restoration "English Restoration")" in 1660\.[\[26]](#cite_note-DB11-26) Several members of the court of King [Charles II](/wiki/Charles_II_of_England,_Scotland_and_Ireland "Charles II of England, Scotland and Ireland") took a strong interest in cricket during that era.[\[27]](#cite_note-27) Gambling on sport became a problem significant enough for [Parliament](/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom "Parliament of the United Kingdom") to pass the 1664 Gambling Act, limiting stakes to [£](/wiki/Pound_sterling "Pound sterling")100, which was, in any case, a colossal sum exceeding the annual income of 99% of the population.[\[26]](#cite_note-DB11-26) Along with [horse racing](/wiki/Horse_racing "Horse racing"), as well as [prizefighting](/wiki/Prizefighting "Prizefighting") and other types of [blood sport](/wiki/Blood_sport "Blood sport"), cricket was perceived to be a gambling sport.[\[28]](#cite_note-28) Rich patrons made matches for high stakes, forming teams in which they engaged the first professional players.[\[29]](#cite_note-RW10-29) By the end of the century, cricket had developed into a major sport that was spreading throughout England and was already being taken abroad by English mariners and colonisers—the earliest reference to cricket overseas is dated 1676\.[\[30]](#cite_note-30) A 1697 newspaper report survives of "a great cricket match" played in Sussex "for fifty guineas apiece", the earliest known contest that is generally considered a [First Class](/wiki/First-class_cricket "First-class cricket") match.[\[31]](#cite_note-31)[\[32]](#cite_note-32)


The patrons and other players from the [gentry](/wiki/Gentry "Gentry") began to classify themselves as "[amateurs](/wiki/Amateur_status_in_first-class_cricket "Amateur status in first-class cricket")"[\[fn 1]](#cite_note-amateur-33) to establish a clear distinction from the professionals, who were invariably members of the [working class](/wiki/Working_class "Working class"), even to the point of having separate changing and dining facilities.[\[33]](#cite_note-34) The gentry, including such high\-ranking nobles as the [Dukes of Richmond](/wiki/Duke_of_Richmond "Duke of Richmond"), exerted their honour code of *[noblesse oblige](/wiki/Noblesse_oblige "Noblesse oblige")* to claim rights of leadership in any sporting contests they took part in, especially as it was necessary for them to play alongside their "social inferiors" if they were to win their bets.[\[34]](#cite_note-DB19-35) In time, a perception took hold that the typical amateur who played in first\-class cricket, until 1962 when amateurism was abolished, was someone with a [public school](/wiki/Public_school_(United_Kingdom) "Public school (United Kingdom)") education who had then gone to one of [Cambridge](/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") or [Oxford University](/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"). Society insisted that such people were "officers and gentlemen" whose destiny was to provide leadership.[\[35]](#cite_note-CW23-36) In a purely financial sense, the cricketing amateur would theoretically claim expenses for playing while his professional counterpart played under contract and was paid a wage or match fee; in practice, many amateurs claimed more than actual expenditure, and the derisive term "shamateur" was coined to describe the practice.[\[36]](#cite_note-37)[\[37]](#cite_note-DB146-38)



### English cricket in the 18th and 19th centuries




[Francis Cotes](/wiki/Francis_Cotes "Francis Cotes"), *The Young Cricketer*, 1768


The game underwent major development in the 18th century to become England's [national sport](/wiki/National_sport "National sport").[\[38]](#cite_note-39) Its success was underwritten by the twin necessities of patronage and betting.[\[39]](#cite_note-40) Cricket was prominent in [London](/wiki/London "London") as early as 1707 and, in the middle years of the century, large crowds flocked to matches on the [Artillery Ground](/wiki/Artillery_Ground "Artillery Ground") in [Finsbury](/wiki/Finsbury "Finsbury").\[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] The [single wicket](/wiki/Single_wicket_cricket "Single wicket cricket") form of the sport attracted huge crowds and wagers to match, its popularity peaking in the [1748 season](/wiki/1748_English_cricket_season "1748 English cricket season").[\[40]](#cite_note-ASW-41) Bowling underwent an evolution around 1760 when bowlers began to [pitch (bounce) the ball](/wiki/Pitched_delivery_bowling "Pitched delivery bowling") instead of rolling or skimming it towards the batter. This caused a revolution in bat design because, to deal with the bouncing ball, it was necessary to introduce the modern straight bat in place of the old "hockey stick" shape.[\[41]](#cite_note-42)\[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]


The [Hambledon Club](/wiki/Hambledon_Club "Hambledon Club") was founded in the 1760s and, for the next twenty years until the formation of [Marylebone Cricket Club](/wiki/Marylebone_Cricket_Club "Marylebone Cricket Club") (MCC) and the opening of [Lord's Old Ground](/wiki/Lord%27s_Old_Ground "Lord's Old Ground") in 1787, Hambledon was both the game's greatest club and its focal point.\[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] MCC quickly became the sport's premier club and the custodian of the *[Laws of Cricket](/wiki/Laws_of_Cricket "Laws of Cricket")*. New Laws introduced in the latter part of the 18th century include the three\-stump wicket and [leg before wicket](/wiki/Leg_before_wicket "Leg before wicket") (lbw).[\[42]](#cite_note-WCA63-43)


The 19th century saw [underarm bowling](/wiki/Underarm_bowling "Underarm bowling") superseded by first [roundarm](/wiki/Roundarm_bowling "Roundarm bowling") and then [overarm bowling](/wiki/Overarm_bowling "Overarm bowling"). Both developments were controversial.[\[43]](#cite_note-44) Organisation of the game at county level led to the creation of the [county clubs](/wiki/County_cricket_club "County cricket club"), starting with [Sussex](/wiki/Sussex_County_Cricket_Club "Sussex County Cricket Club") in 1839\.[\[44]](#cite_note-45) In December 1889, the eight leading county clubs formed the official [County Championship](/wiki/County_Championship "County Championship"), which began in 1890\.[\[45]](#cite_note-AMCS-46)





The first recorded photo of a cricket match taken on 25 July 1857 by [Roger Fenton](/wiki/Roger_Fenton "Roger Fenton")


The most famous player of the 19th century was [W. G. Grace](/wiki/W._G._Grace "W. G. Grace"), who started his long and influential career in 1865\. It was especially during the career of Grace that the distinction between amateurs and professionals became blurred by the existence of players like him who were nominally amateur but, in terms of their financial gain, *de facto* professional. Grace himself was said to have been paid more money for playing cricket than any professional.\[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]


The last two decades before the [First World War](/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") have been called the "[Golden Age of cricket](/wiki/Golden_Age_of_cricket "Golden Age of cricket")". It is a nostalgic name prompted by the collective sense of loss resulting from the war, but the period did produce some great players and memorable matches, especially as organised competition at county and [Test level](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") developed.[\[46]](#cite_note-47)



### Cricket becomes an international sport




The first English team to tour overseas, on board ship to North America, 1859


In 1844, the [first\-ever international match](/wiki/United_States_v_Canada_(1844) "United States v Canada (1844)") took place between what were essentially club teams, from the [United States](/wiki/United_States_national_cricket_team "United States national cricket team") and [Canada](/wiki/Canada_national_cricket_team "Canada national cricket team"), in [Toronto](/wiki/Toronto "Toronto"); Canada won.[\[47]](#cite_note-48)[\[48]](#cite_note-49) In 1859, a team of English players went to North America on [the first overseas tour](/wiki/English_cricket_team_in_North_America_in_1859 "English cricket team in North America in 1859").[\[49]](#cite_note-50) Meanwhile, the [British Empire](/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire") had been instrumental in spreading the game overseas, and by the middle of the 19th century it had become well established in [Australia](/wiki/Australia "Australia"), the [Caribbean](/wiki/Caribbean "Caribbean"), [British India](/wiki/British_India "British India") (which includes present\-day [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan") and [Bangladesh](/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh")), [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand "New Zealand"), [North America](/wiki/North_America "North America") and [South Africa](/wiki/South_Africa "South Africa").[\[50]](#cite_note-51)


In 1862, an English team made the first tour of Australia.[\[51]](#cite_note-52) The first Australian team to travel overseas consisted of [Aboriginal](/wiki/Indigenous_Australians "Indigenous Australians") [stockmen](/wiki/Stockman_(Australia) "Stockman (Australia)") who [toured England in 1868](/wiki/Australian_Aboriginal_cricket_team_in_England_in_1868 "Australian Aboriginal cricket team in England in 1868").[\[52]](#cite_note-53)


In 1876–77, an [England team](/wiki/England_cricket_team "England cricket team") took part in what was retrospectively recognised as the first\-ever [Test match](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") at the [Melbourne Cricket Ground](/wiki/Melbourne_Cricket_Ground "Melbourne Cricket Ground") against [Australia](/wiki/Australia_national_cricket_team "Australia national cricket team").[\[53]](#cite_note-54) The rivalry between England and Australia gave birth to [The Ashes](/wiki/The_Ashes "The Ashes") in 1882, which remains Test cricket's most famous contest.[\[54]](#cite_note-55) Test cricket began to expand in 1888–89 when [South Africa](/wiki/South_Africa_national_cricket_team "South Africa national cricket team") played England.[\[55]](#cite_note-56)



### Cricket in the 20th century




[Don Bradman](/wiki/Don_Bradman "Don Bradman") of [Australia](/wiki/Australia_national_cricket_team "Australia national cricket team") had a record Test [batting average](/wiki/Batting_average "Batting average") of 99\.94\.


The inter\-war years were dominated by [Australia](/wiki/Australia_national_cricket_team "Australia national cricket team")'s [Don Bradman](/wiki/Don_Bradman "Don Bradman"), statistically the greatest Test batter of all time. To curb his dominance, England employed [bodyline](/wiki/Bodyline "Bodyline") tactics during the [1932–33 Ashes series](/wiki/English_cricket_team_in_Australia_in_1932%E2%80%9333 "English cricket team in Australia in 1932–33"). These involved bowling at the body of the batter and setting a field, resulting in batters having to choose between being hit or risk getting out. This series moved cricket from a game to a matter of [national importance](/wiki/National_sport "National sport"), with diplomatic cables being passed between the two countries over the incident.[\[56]](#cite_note-57)


During this time, the number of Test nations continued to grow, with the [West Indies](/wiki/West_Indies_Cricket_Team "West Indies Cricket Team"), [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand_Cricket_Team "New Zealand Cricket Team") and [India](/wiki/Indian_Cricket_Team "Indian Cricket Team") being admitted as full Test members within a four\-year period from 1928 to 1932\.


An enforced break during the [Second World War](/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War") stopped Test Cricket for a time, although the [Partition of India](/wiki/Partition_of_India "Partition of India") caused [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan_Cricket_Team "Pakistan Cricket Team") to gain Test status in 1952\. As teams began to travel more, the game quickly grew from 500 tests in 84 years to 1000 within the next 23\.


Cricket entered a new era in 1963 when English counties introduced the [limited overs](/wiki/Limited_overs_cricket "Limited overs cricket") variant.[\[57]](#cite_note-58) As it was sure to produce a result, limited overs cricket was lucrative, and the number of matches increased.[\[58]](#cite_note-59) The first [Limited Overs International](/wiki/Limited_Overs_International "Limited Overs International") was played in 1971, and the governing [International Cricket Council](/wiki/International_Cricket_Council "International Cricket Council") (ICC), seeing its potential, staged the first limited overs [Cricket World Cup](/wiki/Cricket_World_Cup "Cricket World Cup") in [1975](/wiki/1975_Cricket_World_Cup "1975 Cricket World Cup").[\[59]](#cite_note-60)


[Sri Lanka](/wiki/Sri_Lankan_Cricket_Team "Sri Lankan Cricket Team") joined the ranks in 1982\. Meanwhile, South Africa was banned by the ICC due to [apartheid](/wiki/Apartheid "Apartheid") from 1970 until 1992\. 1992 also brought about the introduction of the [Zimbabwe team](/wiki/Zimbabwe_Cricket_Team "Zimbabwe Cricket Team").[\[60]](#cite_note-61)



### Cricket in the 21st century




The [Indian Premier League](/wiki/Indian_Premier_League "Indian Premier League") (IPL) was launched in 2008\. It has become one of the richest sports leagues in the world, and has greatly increased the importance of T20 cricket and franchise leagues.[\[61]](#cite_note-62)


The 21st century brought with it the [Bangladesh Team](/wiki/Bangladesh_Cricket_Team "Bangladesh Cricket Team"), who made their Test debut in 2000\. The game itself also grew, with a new format made up of 20\-over innings being created. This format, called [T20 cricket](/wiki/T20_cricket "T20 cricket"), quickly became a highly popular format, putting the longer formats at risk. The new shorter format also introduced [franchise](/wiki/Franchise_(sport) "Franchise (sport)") cricket, with new tournaments like the Indian Premier League and the Australian [Big Bash League](/wiki/Big_Bash_League "Big Bash League"). The ICC has selected the T20 format as cricket's growth format, and has introduced a [T20 World Cup](/wiki/T20_World_Cup "T20 World Cup") which is played every two years;[\[62]](#cite_note-63) T20 cricket has also been increasingly accepted into major events such as the [Asian Games](/wiki/Asian_Games "Asian Games").[\[63]](#cite_note-64) The resultant growth has seen cricket's fanbase cross one billion people, with [90% of them](/wiki/Cricket_in_South_Asia "Cricket in South Asia") in South Asia.[\[2]](#cite_note-:0-2) T20's success has also spawned even [shorter formats](/wiki/Short_form_cricket "Short form cricket"), such as [10\-over cricket](/wiki/T10_cricket "T10 cricket") (T10\) and [100\-ball cricket](/wiki/100-ball_cricket "100-ball cricket"), though not without controversy.[\[64]](#cite_note-65)


Outside factors have also taken their toll on cricket. For example, the [2008 Mumbai attacks](/wiki/2008_Mumbai_attacks "2008 Mumbai attacks") led India and Pakistan to suspend their [bilateral series](/wiki/India%E2%80%93Pakistan_cricket_rivalry "India–Pakistan cricket rivalry") indefinitely. The [2009 attack on the Sri Lankan team](/wiki/2009_attack_on_the_Sri_Lanka_national_cricket_team "2009 attack on the Sri Lanka national cricket team") during their tour of Pakistan led to Pakistan being unable to host matches until 2019\.[\[65]](#cite_note-66)[\[66]](#cite_note-67)[\[67]](#cite_note-68)[\[68]](#cite_note-69)


In 2017, [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan_national_cricket_team "Afghanistan national cricket team") and [Ireland](/wiki/Ireland_cricket_team "Ireland cricket team") became the 11th and 12th Test nations.[\[69]](#cite_note-70)[\[70]](#cite_note-71)



Laws and gameplay
-----------------


Main article: [Laws of Cricket](/wiki/Laws_of_Cricket "Laws of Cricket")


A typical [cricket field](/wiki/Cricket_field "Cricket field")


In cricket, the rules of the game are codified in *[The Laws of Cricket](/wiki/The_Laws_of_Cricket "The Laws of Cricket")* (hereinafter called "the Laws"), which has a global remit. There are 42 Laws (always written with a capital "L"). The earliest known version of the code was drafted in 1744, and since 1788, it has been owned and maintained by its custodian, the [Marylebone Cricket Club](/wiki/Marylebone_Cricket_Club "Marylebone Cricket Club") (MCC) in [London](/wiki/London "London").[\[71]](#cite_note-Laws-72)



### Playing area


Main articles: [Cricket field](/wiki/Cricket_field "Cricket field"), [Cricket pitch](/wiki/Cricket_pitch "Cricket pitch"), [Crease (cricket)](/wiki/Crease_(cricket) "Crease (cricket)"), and [Wicket](/wiki/Wicket "Wicket")
Cricket is a [bat\-and\-ball game](/wiki/Bat-and-ball_games "Bat-and-ball games") played on a [cricket field](/wiki/Cricket_field "Cricket field") (see image of cricket pitch and creases) between two teams of eleven players each.[\[72]](#cite_note-Law1-73) The field is usually circular or oval in shape, and the edge of the playing area is marked by a [boundary](/wiki/Boundary_(cricket) "Boundary (cricket)"), which may be a fence, part of the stands, a rope, a painted line, or a combination of these; the boundary must if possible be marked along its entire length.[\[73]](#cite_note-74)


In the approximate centre of the field is a rectangular [pitch](/wiki/Cricket_pitch "Cricket pitch") (see image, below) on which a wooden target called a [wicket](/wiki/Wicket "Wicket") is sited at each end; the wickets are placed 22 yards (20 m) apart.[\[74]](#cite_note-75) The pitch is a flat surface 10 feet (3\.0 m) wide, with very short grass that tends to be worn away as the game progresses (cricket can also be played on artificial surfaces, notably matting). Each wicket is made of three wooden [stumps](/wiki/Stump_(cricket) "Stump (cricket)") topped by two [bails](/wiki/Bail_(cricket) "Bail (cricket)").[\[75]](#cite_note-76)





Cricket pitch and creases


As illustrated, the pitch is marked at each end with four white painted lines: a [bowling crease](/wiki/Crease_(cricket)#Bowling_crease "Crease (cricket)"), a [popping crease](/wiki/Crease_(cricket)#Popping_crease "Crease (cricket)") and two [return creases](/wiki/Crease_(cricket)#Return_crease "Crease (cricket)"). The three stumps are aligned centrally on the bowling crease, which is eight feet eight inches long. The popping crease is drawn four feet in front of the bowling crease and parallel to it; although it is drawn as a 12 ft (3\.7 m) line (six feet on either side of the wicket), it is, in fact, unlimited in length. The return creases are drawn at right angles to the popping crease so that they intersect the ends of the bowling crease; each return crease is drawn as an 8 ft (2\.4 m) line, so that it extends four feet behind the bowling crease, but is also, in fact, unlimited in length.[\[76]](#cite_note-77)



### Match structure


Main article: [Innings](/wiki/Innings "Innings")
Before a match begins, the team [captains](/wiki/Captain_(cricket) "Captain (cricket)") (who are also players) [toss a coin](/wiki/Toss_(cricket) "Toss (cricket)") to decide which team will [bat](/wiki/Batting_(cricket) "Batting (cricket)") first and so take the first [innings](/wiki/Innings "Innings").[\[77]](#cite_note-inns-78) "Innings" is the term used for each phase of play in the match.[\[77]](#cite_note-inns-78) In each innings, one team bats, attempting to [score](/wiki/Scoring_(cricket) "Scoring (cricket)") [runs](/wiki/Run_(cricket) "Run (cricket)"), while the other team [bowls](/wiki/Bowling_(cricket) "Bowling (cricket)") and [fields](/wiki/Fielding_(cricket) "Fielding (cricket)") the [ball](/wiki/Cricket_ball "Cricket ball"), attempting to restrict the scoring and [dismiss](/wiki/Dismissal_(cricket) "Dismissal (cricket)") the batters.[\[78]](#cite_note-79)[\[79]](#cite_note-80) When the first innings ends, the teams change roles; there can be two to four innings depending upon the type of match. A match with four scheduled innings is played over three to five days; a match with two scheduled innings is usually completed in a single day.[\[77]](#cite_note-inns-78) During an innings, all eleven members of the fielding team take the field, but usually only two members of the batting team are on the field at any given time.[\[a]](#cite_note-83) The order of batters is usually announced just before the match, but it can be varied.[\[72]](#cite_note-Law1-73)


The main objective of each team is to score more runs than their opponents, but in some forms of cricket, it is also necessary to dismiss all but one of the opposition batters (making their team 'all out') in their final innings in order to win the match, which would otherwise be [drawn](/wiki/Result_(cricket)#Draw "Result (cricket)") (not ending with a winner or tie.)[\[82]](#cite_note-result-84)



### Clothing and equipment


Main article: [Cricket clothing and equipment](/wiki/Cricket_clothing_and_equipment "Cricket clothing and equipment")


English cricketer [W. G. Grace](/wiki/W._G._Grace "W. G. Grace") "taking guard" in 1883\. His pads and bat are very similar to those used today. The gloves have evolved somewhat. Many modern players use more defensive equipment than were available to Grace, most notably helmets and arm guards.


The [wicket\-keeper](/wiki/Wicket-keeper "Wicket-keeper") (a specialised fielder behind the batter) and the batters wear protective gear because of the hardness of the ball, which can be delivered at speeds of more than 145 kilometres per hour (90 mph) and presents a major health and safety concern. Protective clothing includes [pads](/wiki/Batting_pads "Batting pads") (designed to protect the knees and shins), [batting gloves](/wiki/Batting_gloves "Batting gloves") or [wicket\-keeper's gloves](/wiki/Wicket-keeper%27s_gloves "Wicket-keeper's gloves") for the hands, a [safety helmet](/wiki/Helmet_(cricket) "Helmet (cricket)") for the head, and a [box](/wiki/Box_(cricket) "Box (cricket)") for male players inside the trousers (to protect the [crotch](/wiki/Crotch "Crotch") area).[\[83]](#cite_note-AppD-85) Some batters wear additional padding inside their shirts and trousers such as thigh pads, arm pads, rib protectors and shoulder pads. The only fielders allowed to wear protective gear are those in positions very close to the batter (i.e., if they are alongside or in front of him), but they cannot wear gloves or external leg guards.[\[84]](#cite_note-Law41-86)


Subject to certain variations, on\-field clothing generally includes a collared shirt with short or long sleeves; long trousers; woolen pullover (if needed); [cricket cap](/wiki/Cricket_cap "Cricket cap") (for fielding) or a safety helmet; and spiked shoes or boots to increase traction. The [kit](/wiki/Cricket_kits "Cricket kits") is traditionally all white, and this remains the case in Test and first\-class cricket, but in limited overs cricket, team colours are now worn instead.[\[85]](#cite_note-87)



#### Bat and ball


Main articles: [Cricket bat](/wiki/Cricket_bat "Cricket bat") and [Cricket ball](/wiki/Cricket_ball "Cricket ball")
Two types of [cricket ball](/wiki/Cricket_ball "Cricket ball"), both of the same size:
i) A used white ball. White balls are mainly used in [limited overs cricket](/wiki/Limited_overs_cricket "Limited overs cricket"), especially in matches played at night, under [floodlights](/wiki/Floodlights "Floodlights") (left).  




ii) A used red ball. Red balls are used in [Test cricket](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket"), [first\-class cricket](/wiki/First-class_cricket "First-class cricket") and some other forms of cricket (right).
The essence of the sport is that a [bowler](/wiki/Bowling_(cricket) "Bowling (cricket)") [delivers](/wiki/Delivery_(cricket) "Delivery (cricket)") (i.e., bowls) the [ball](/wiki/Cricket_ball "Cricket ball") from their end of the [pitch](/wiki/Cricket_pitch "Cricket pitch") towards the [batter](/wiki/Batting_(cricket) "Batting (cricket)") who, armed with a [bat](/wiki/Cricket_bat "Cricket bat"), is "on strike" at the other end (see next sub\-section: *[Basic gameplay](#Basic_gameplay:_bowler_to_batter)*).


The [bat](/wiki/Cricket_bat "Cricket bat") is made of wood, usually *[Salix alba](/wiki/Salix_alba "Salix alba")* (white willow), and has the shape of a blade topped by a cylindrical handle. The blade must not be more than 4\.25 inches (10\.8 cm) wide and the total length of the bat not more than 38 inches (97 cm). There is no standard for the weight, which is usually between 2 lb 7 oz and 3 lb (1\.1 and 1\.4 kg).[\[86]](#cite_note-Law6-88)[\[87]](#cite_note-89)


The [ball](/wiki/Cricket_ball "Cricket ball") is a hard leather\-seamed [spheroid](/wiki/Spheroid "Spheroid"), with a circumference of 9 inches (23 cm). The ball has a "seam": six rows of stitches attaching the leather shell of the ball to the string and cork interior. The seam on a new ball is prominent and helps the bowler propel it in a less predictable manner. During matches, the quality of the ball deteriorates to a point where it is no longer usable; during the course of this deterioration, its behaviour in flight will change and can influence the outcome of the match. Players will, therefore, attempt to modify the ball's behaviour by modifying its physical properties. Polishing the ball and wetting it with sweat or saliva was legal, even when the polishing was deliberately done on one side only to increase the ball's [swing through the air](/wiki/Swing_bowling "Swing bowling"). The use of saliva has since been made illegal due to the [COVID\-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic").[\[88]](#cite_note-90) The acts of rubbing other substances into the ball, scratching the surface or picking at the seams constitute illegal [ball tampering](/wiki/Ball_tampering "Ball tampering").[\[89]](#cite_note-Law5-91)

##### Overs


Main article: [Over (cricket)](/wiki/Over_(cricket) "Over (cricket)")
The Laws state that, throughout an innings, "the ball shall be bowled from each end alternately in overs of 6 balls".[\[91]](#cite_note-Law22-93) The name "over" came about because the umpire calls "Over!" when six legal balls (deliveries) have been bowled. At this point, another bowler is deployed at the other end, and the fielding side changes ends while the batters do not. A bowler cannot bowl two successive overs, although a bowler can (and usually does) bowl alternate overs, from the same end, for several overs which are termed a "spell"; if the captain wants a bowler to "change ends", another bowler must temporarily fill in so that the change is not immediate. The batters do not change ends at the end of the over, and so the one who was non\-striker is now the striker and vice versa. The umpires also change positions so that the one who was at "[square leg](/wiki/Square_leg "Square leg")" now stands behind the wicket at the nonstriker's end and vice versa.[\[91]](#cite_note-Law22-93)

Of the eleven fielders, three are in shot in the image above. The other eight are elsewhere on the field, their positions determined on a tactical basis by the captain or the bowler. Fielders often change position between deliveries, again as directed by the captain or bowler.[\[84]](#cite_note-Law41-86)


If a fielder is injured or becomes ill during a match, a [substitute](/wiki/Substitute_(cricket) "Substitute (cricket)") is allowed to field instead of the aforementioned fielder, but the substitute cannot bowl or act as a captain, except in the case of concussion substitutes in international cricket.[\[81]](#cite_note-ICCTestPlayCond-82) The substitute leaves the field when the injured player is fit to return.[\[92]](#cite_note-Law2-94) The *Laws of Cricket* were updated in 2017 to allow substitutes to act as wicket\-keepers.[\[93]](#cite_note-95)



#### Batting and scoring


Main articles: [Batting (cricket)](/wiki/Batting_(cricket) "Batting (cricket)"), [Run (cricket)](/wiki/Run_(cricket) "Run (cricket)"), and [Extra (cricket)](/wiki/Extra_(cricket) "Extra (cricket)")


The directions in which a right\-handed batter, facing down the page, intends to send the ball when playing various cricketing shots. The diagram for a [left\-handed](/wiki/Left-handed "Left-handed") batter is a [mirror image](/wiki/Mirror_image "Mirror image") of this one.


Batters take turns to bat via a [batting order](/wiki/Batting_order_(cricket) "Batting order (cricket)") which is decided beforehand by the team captain and presented to the umpires, though the order remains flexible when the captain officially nominates the team.[\[72]](#cite_note-Law1-73) Substitute batters are generally not allowed,[\[92]](#cite_note-Law2-94) except in the case of concussion substitutes in international cricket.[\[81]](#cite_note-ICCTestPlayCond-82)


In order to begin batting the batter first adopts a batting stance. Standardly, this involves adopting a slight crouch with the feet pointing across the front of the wicket, looking in the direction of the bowler, and holding the bat so it passes over the feet and so its tip can rest on the ground near to the toes of the back foot.[\[94]](#cite_note-96)


A skilled batter can use a wide array of "shots" or "strokes" in both defensive and attacking mode. The idea is to hit the ball to the best effect with the flat surface of the bat's blade. If the ball touches the side of the bat, it is called an "[edge](/wiki/Edge_(cricket) "Edge (cricket)")". The batter does not have to play a shot and can allow the ball to go through to the [wicket\-keeper](/wiki/Wicket-keeper "Wicket-keeper"). Equally, the batter does not have to attempt a run when hitting the ball with their bat. Batters do not always seek to hit the ball as hard as possible, and a good player can score runs by simply making a deft stroke with a turn of the wrists, or by simply "blocking" the ball but directing it away from fielders so that the player has time to take a run. A wide variety of shots are played, the batter's repertoire including strokes named according to the style of swing and the direction aimed: e.g., "[cut](/wiki/Cut_(cricket) "Cut (cricket)")", "drive", "hook", and "pull".[\[95]](#cite_note-97)





[Sachin Tendulkar](/wiki/Sachin_Tendulkar "Sachin Tendulkar") is the only player to have scored one hundred international [centuries](/wiki/Century_(cricket) "Century (cricket)").


The batter on strike (i.e., the "striker") must prevent the ball from hitting the wicket and try to score [runs](/wiki/Run_(cricket) "Run (cricket)") by hitting the ball with their bat so that the batter and their partner have time to switch places, with each of them running from one end of the pitch to the other before the fielding side can return the ball and attempt a [run out](/wiki/Run_out "Run out") (throwing the ball at one of the wickets before the run is scored.) To register a run, both runners must touch the [ground](/wiki/Batter%27s_ground "Batter's ground") behind the popping crease with either their bats or their bodies (the batters carry their bats as they run) before a fielder can throw the ball at the nearby wicket. Each completed run increments the score of both the team and the striker.[\[96]](#cite_note-Law18-98)


The decision to attempt a run is ideally made by the batter who has the better view of the ball's progress, and this is communicated by calling, usually "yes", "no" or "wait". More than one run can be scored from a single hit. Hits worth one to three runs are common, but the size of the field is such that it is usually difficult to run four or more.[\[96]](#cite_note-Law18-98) To compensate for this, hits that [reach the boundary](/wiki/Boundary_(scoring) "Boundary (scoring)") of the field are automatically awarded four runs if the ball touches the ground *en route* to the boundary or six runs if the ball clears the boundary without touching the ground within the boundary. In these cases the batters do not need to run.[\[97]](#cite_note-Law19-99) Hits for five are unusual and generally rely on the help of "overthrows" by a fielder returning the ball.





Batters attempting a run while a fielder awaits a throw.


If an odd number of runs is scored by the striker, the two batters have changed ends, and the one who was non\-striker is now the striker. Only the striker can score individual runs, but all runs are added to the team's total.[\[96]](#cite_note-Law18-98)


Additional runs can be gained by the batting team as [extras](/wiki/Extra_(cricket) "Extra (cricket)") (called "sundries" in Australia) due to errors made by the fielding side. This is achieved in four ways: [no\-ball](/wiki/No-ball "No-ball"), a penalty of one extra conceded by the bowler if they break the rules (often by failing to bowl the ball before their front foot passes the popping crease at their end);[\[98]](#cite_note-Law24-100) [wide](/wiki/Wide_(cricket) "Wide (cricket)"), a penalty of one extra conceded by the bowler if they bowl so that the ball is out of the batter's reach;[\[99]](#cite_note-Law25a-101) [bye](/wiki/Bye_(cricket) "Bye (cricket)"), an extra awarded if the batter misses the ball and it goes past the wicket\-keeper and gives the batters time to run in the conventional way;[\[100]](#cite_note-Law26-102) and [leg bye](/wiki/Leg_bye "Leg bye"), as for a bye except that the ball has hit the batter's body, though not their bat.[\[100]](#cite_note-Law26-102) If the bowler has bowled an illegal delivery (i.e., a no\-ball or a wide), the bowler's team incurs an additional penalty because that ball (i.e., delivery) has to be bowled again, and hence the batting side has the opportunity to score more runs from this extra ball. In addition, the ways in which the batters can be dismissed on an illegal delivery greatly narrow down; in the case of a no\-ball, which is the more egregious type of illegal delivery, the only common way in which the batters can be dismissed is by being run out.[\[98]](#cite_note-Law24-100)[\[99]](#cite_note-Law25a-101)



#### Dismissals


Main article: [Dismissal (cricket)](/wiki/Dismissal_(cricket) "Dismissal (cricket)")
Most common dismissals involve the wickets, such as when the ball is bowled at the striker's wicket.
There are nine ways in which a batter can be dismissed: five relatively common and four extremely rare. When a batter is dismissed, they are said to have 'lost their wicket', and are barred from batting again in that innings; their team is also said to have 'lost a wicket'. Once a team has lost 10 wickets, its innings is over. The common forms of dismissal are [bowled](/wiki/Bowled "Bowled") (when the striker fails to prevent a delivery from hitting their wicket),[\[101]](#cite_note-Law30-103) [caught](/wiki/Caught "Caught") (when a ball struck by the bat is caught by a fielder before it hits the ground),[\[102]](#cite_note-Law32-104) [leg before wicket](/wiki/Leg_before_wicket "Leg before wicket") (lbw \- when the striker's body 'unfairly' prevents a delivery from hitting the wicket),[\[103]](#cite_note-Law36-105) [run out](/wiki/Run_out "Run out") (generally when the ball is thrown at a wicket by a fielder while the batters are running between the wickets),[\[104]](#cite_note-Law38-106) and [stumped](/wiki/Stumped "Stumped") (a special type of run out).[\[105]](#cite_note-Law39-107) Rare methods are [hit wicket](/wiki/Hit_wicket "Hit wicket") (a striker hitting their own wicket),[\[106]](#cite_note-Law35-108) [hit the ball twice](/wiki/Hit_the_ball_twice "Hit the ball twice"),[\[107]](#cite_note-Law34-109) [obstructing the field](/wiki/Obstructing_the_field "Obstructing the field"),[\[108]](#cite_note-Law37-110) and [timed out](/wiki/Timed_out "Timed out") (a batter failing to enter the field in a timely manner).[\[109]](#cite_note-Law31-111) The Laws state that the fielding team, usually the bowler in practice, must appeal for a dismissal before the umpire can give their decision. If the batter is out, the umpire raises a forefinger and says "Out!"; otherwise, the umpire will shake their head and say "Not out".[\[110]](#cite_note-Law27-112) There is, effectively, a tenth method of dismissal, [retired out](/wiki/Retired_out "Retired out") (self\-dismissal \- generally permanent except in cases of injury), which is not an on\-field dismissal as such but rather a retrospective one for which no fielder is credited.[\[111]](#cite_note-Law2RO-113)

Most bowlers are considered specialists in that they are selected for the team because of their skill as a bowler, although some are all\-rounders, and even specialist batters bowl occasionally. These specialists bowl "spells" that are generally 4 to 8 overs long in order not to physically exhaust the bowler, cause muscle strain and stress the skeleton. The rules prevent a single bowler from bowling consecutive overs, resulting in at least two bowlers alternating each over. If the captain wants a bowler to "change ends", another bowler must temporarily fill in so that the change is not immediate.[\[91]](#cite_note-Law22-93) The action of bowling the ball is akin to throwing, with the caveat that a bowler's elbow extension is almost entirely restricted, resulting in most bowlers maintaining a straight arm when releasing the ball during their delivery stride. Additionally, while the bowler is not required to pitch (bounce) the ball, a full toss (non\-bouncing) delivery that reaches the striker above waist height is penalised as a [no\-ball](/wiki/No-ball "No-ball").


A bowler reaches their delivery stride by means of a "run\-up", and an over is deemed to have begun when the bowler starts their run\-up for the first delivery of that over, the ball then being "in play".[\[91]](#cite_note-Law22-93) Fast bowlers, or pacemen, need momentum, taking a lengthy run up, while bowlers with a slow delivery take no more than a couple of steps before bowling. The fastest bowlers can deliver the ball at a speed of over 145 kilometres per hour (90 mph), and they sometimes rely on sheer speed to try to defeat the batter, who is forced to react very quickly.[\[112]](#cite_note-pace-114) Other fast bowlers rely on a mixture of speed and guile by making the ball [seam](/wiki/Seam_bowling "Seam bowling") or [swing](/wiki/Swing_bowling "Swing bowling") (i.e., curve) in flight. This type of delivery can deceive a batter into miscuing their shot, for example, so that the ball just touches the edge of the bat and can then be "caught behind" by the wicket\-keeper or a slip fielder.[\[112]](#cite_note-pace-114) At the other end of the bowling scale is the [spin bowler](/wiki/Spin_bowling "Spin bowling"), who bowls at a relatively slow pace and relies entirely on guile to deceive the batter. A spinner will often "buy their wicket" by "tossing one up" (in a slower, steeper [parabolic](/wiki/Parabolic_trajectory "Parabolic trajectory") path) to lure the batter into making a poor shot. The batter has to be very wary of such deliveries, as the batter is often "flighted" or spun so that the ball will not behave quite as the batter expects it to, and the batter could be "trapped" into getting themself out. Accidental full toss deliveries can also get wickets, as the failure of the ball to bounce can surprise a batsman or induce a poor stroke in an effort to punish the poor delivery with a boundary hit.[\[113]](#cite_note-spin-115) In between the pacemen and the spinners are the medium\-paced seamers, who rely on persistent accuracy to try to contain the rate of scoring and wear down the batter's concentration.[\[112]](#cite_note-pace-114)



#### Specialist roles


Main articles: [Captain (cricket)](/wiki/Captain_(cricket) "Captain (cricket)") and [Wicket\-keeper](/wiki/Wicket-keeper "Wicket-keeper")
The captain is often the most experienced player in the team, certainly the most tactically astute, and can possess any of the main skillsets as a batter, a bowler or a wicket\-keeper. Within the Laws, the captain has certain responsibilities in terms of nominating their players to the umpires before the match and ensuring that the captain's players conduct themselves "within the spirit and traditions of the game as well as within the Laws".[\[72]](#cite_note-Law1-73)


The wicket\-keeper (sometimes called simply the "keeper") is a specialist fielder subject to various rules within the Laws about their equipment and demeanour. The wicket\-keeper is the only member of the fielding side who can effect a [stumping](/wiki/Stumped "Stumped") and is the only one permitted to wear gloves and external leg guards.[\[114]](#cite_note-Law40-116)


Depending on their primary skills, the other ten players in the team tend to be classified as specialist batters or specialist bowlers. Generally, a team will include five or six specialist batters, and four or five specialist bowlers, plus the wicket\-keeper.[\[115]](#cite_note-117)[\[116]](#cite_note-118)



### Match closure


Main article: [Result (cricket)](/wiki/Result_(cricket) "Result (cricket)")


Match results are sometimes determined by a final dismissal, either because the batting team's last wicket falls, or in a limited\-overs match, the chance to score is denied on the final delivery.


There are a number of ways that a cricket match can end and its result be described, depending on whether the team batting first or last wins as well as the format of the game.


If the team batting last is 'all out' having scored fewer runs than their opponents, they are said to have "lost by *n* runs" (where *n* is the difference between the aggregate number of runs scored by the teams). If the team that bats last scores enough runs to win, it is said to have "won by *n* wickets", where *n* is the number of wickets left to fall (batters yet to be dismissed) until the team would have been all out. For example, a team that passes its opponents' total having lost six wickets (i.e., six of their batters have been dismissed) wins the match "by four wickets", since the team would only have been prevented from scoring the winning runs if four more of its batters had been dismissed, which would have resulted in all but one of its eleven batters being dismissed.[\[82]](#cite_note-result-84)


In a two\-innings\-a\-side match, one team's combined first and second innings total may be less than the other side's first innings total. The team with the greater score is then said to have "won by an innings and *n* runs" and does not need to bat again: *n* is the difference between the two teams' aggregate scores. If the team batting last is all out and both sides have scored the same number of runs, then the match is a [tie](/wiki/Result_(cricket)#Tie "Result (cricket)"); this result is quite rare in matches of two innings a side with only 62 happening in [first\-class matches](/wiki/First-class_cricket "First-class cricket") from the earliest known instance in 1741 until January 2017\. In the [traditional form](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") of the game, if the time allotted for the match expires before either side can win, then the game is declared a [draw](/wiki/Result_(cricket)#Draw "Result (cricket)").[\[82]](#cite_note-result-84)


If the match has only a single innings per side, then usually a maximum number of overs applies to each innings. Such a match is called a "[limited overs](/wiki/Limited_overs_cricket "Limited overs cricket")" or "one\-day" match, and the side scoring more runs wins regardless of the number of wickets lost, so that a draw cannot occur. In some cases, ties are broken by having each team bat for a one\-over innings known as a [Super Over](/wiki/Super_Over "Super Over"); subsequent Super Overs may be played if the first Super Over ends in a tie. If this kind of match is temporarily interrupted by bad weather, then a complex mathematical formula, known as the [Duckworth–Lewis–Stern method](/wiki/Duckworth%E2%80%93Lewis%E2%80%93Stern_method "Duckworth–Lewis–Stern method") after its developers, is often used to recalculate a new target score. A one\-day match can also be declared a "no\-result" if fewer than a previously agreed number of overs have been bowled by either team, in circumstances that make normal resumption of play impossible, for example, wet weather.[\[82]](#cite_note-result-84)


In all forms of cricket, the umpires can abandon the match if bad light or rain makes it impossible to continue.[\[117]](#cite_note-Law3-119) There have been instances of entire matches, even [Test matches](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") scheduled to be played over five days, being lost to bad weather without a ball being bowled, for example, the third Test of the 1970/71 series in Australia.[\[118]](#cite_note-120)



#### Innings


Main article: [Innings](/wiki/Innings "Innings")


The final innings of the match generally ends when the batting team either scores the winning runs (known as reaching its target score) or runs out of "resources" (either losing 10 wickets or running out of time/overs to bat) and thus can not win.


The innings (ending with 's' in both singular and plural form) is the term used for each phase of play during a match. Depending on the type of match being played, each team has either one or two innings. Sometimes all eleven members of the batting side take a turn to bat but, for various reasons, an innings can end before they have all done so. The innings terminates if the batting team is "all out", a term defined by the Laws: "At the [fall of a wicket](/wiki/Dismissal_(cricket) "Dismissal (cricket)") or the retirement of a batter, further balls remain to be bowled but no further batter is available to come in".[\[77]](#cite_note-inns-78) In this situation, one of the batters has not been dismissed and is termed [not out](/wiki/Not_out "Not out"); this is because he has no partners left and there must always be two active batters while the innings is in progress.


An innings may end early while there are still two not out batters:[\[77]](#cite_note-inns-78)



* the batting team's [captain](/wiki/Captain_(cricket) "Captain (cricket)") [may declare the innings closed](/wiki/Declaration_and_forfeiture "Declaration and forfeiture"), even though some of the captain's players have not had a turn to bat: this is a tactical decision by the captain, usually because the captain believes that their team have scored sufficient runs and need time to dismiss the opposition in their innings
* the set number of [overs](/wiki/Over_(cricket) "Over (cricket)") (i.e., in a limited overs match) have been bowled
* the match has ended prematurely due to bad weather or running out of time
* in the final innings of the match, the batting side has reached its target (i.e., scored more runs than the opposition) and won the game.


### Umpires and scorers


Main articles: [Umpire (cricket)](/wiki/Umpire_(cricket) "Umpire (cricket)"), [Scoring (cricket)](/wiki/Scoring_(cricket) "Scoring (cricket)"), and [Cricket statistics](/wiki/Cricket_statistics "Cricket statistics")


An umpire signals a decision to the scorers.




The [Adelaide Oval](/wiki/Adelaide_Oval "Adelaide Oval") cricket scoreboard during an [Ashes Test](/wiki/The_Ashes "The Ashes") in Australia


The game on the field is regulated by the two [umpires](/wiki/Umpire_(cricket) "Umpire (cricket)"), one of whom stands behind the wicket at the bowler's end and the other in a position called "square leg", which is about 15–20 m (49–66 ft) away from the batter on strike and in line with the popping crease on which that umpire is taking guard. The umpires have several responsibilities, including adjudication on whether a ball has been correctly bowled (i.e., not a [no\-ball](/wiki/No-ball "No-ball") or a [wide](/wiki/Wide_(cricket) "Wide (cricket)")); when a run is scored; whether a batter is out (the fielding side must first [appeal](/wiki/Appeal_(cricket) "Appeal (cricket)") to the umpire, usually with the phrase "How's that?" or "Howzat?"); when intervals start and end; and the suitability of the pitch, field and weather for playing the game. The umpires are authorised to interrupt or even abandon a match due to circumstances likely to endanger the players, such as a damp pitch or deterioration of the light.[\[117]](#cite_note-Law3-119)


Off the field in televised matches, there is usually a [third umpire](/wiki/Third_umpire "Third umpire") who can make decisions on certain incidents with the aid of video evidence. The third umpire is mandatory under the playing conditions for [Test](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") and [Limited Overs International](/wiki/Limited_Overs_International "Limited Overs International") matches played between two ICC full member countries. These matches also have a [match referee](/wiki/Match_referee "Match referee") whose job is to ensure that play is within the Laws and the spirit of the game.[\[117]](#cite_note-Law3-119)


The match details, including runs and dismissals, are recorded by two official [scorers](/wiki/Scoring_(cricket) "Scoring (cricket)"), one representing each team. The scorers are directed by the hand signals of an umpire (see image, right). For example, the umpire raises a forefinger to signal that the batter is out (has been dismissed); the umpire raises both arms above their head if the batter has hit the ball for six runs. The scorers are required by the Laws to record all runs scored, wickets taken, and overs bowled; in practice, they also note significant amounts of additional data relating to the game.[\[119]](#cite_note-121)


A match's [statistics](/wiki/Cricket_statistics "Cricket statistics") are summarised on a [scorecard](/wiki/Cricket_scorecard "Cricket scorecard"). Prior to the popularisation of scorecards, most scoring was done by men sitting on vantage points cuttings notches on [tally sticks](/wiki/Tally_stick "Tally stick"), and runs were originally called notches.[\[120]](#cite_note-122) According to historian [Rowland Bowen](/wiki/Rowland_Bowen "Rowland Bowen"), the earliest known scorecard templates were introduced in 1776 by T. Pratt of [Sevenoaks](/wiki/Sevenoaks "Sevenoaks") and soon came into general use.[\[121]](#cite_note-123) It is believed that scorecards were printed and sold at [Lord's](/wiki/Lord%27s "Lord's") for the first time in 1846\.[\[122]](#cite_note-124)


Scores are displayed differently depending on location, although it is standard to show how many wickets have been lost and how many runs a team has made. Within Australia, the format is Wickets/Runs, while in the rest of the world, the format is Runs/Wickets. For example, a score of 125 runs with 4 wickets lost would be displayed as 4/125 or 125/4, respectively.[\[123]](#cite_note-125)



### Spirit of the Game


Main article: [Laws of Cricket](/wiki/Laws_of_Cricket "Laws of Cricket")
Besides observing the Laws, cricketers must respect the "Spirit of Cricket", a concept encompassing [sportsmanship](/wiki/Sportsmanship "Sportsmanship"), fair play and mutual respect. This spirit has long been considered an integral part of the sport but is only nebulously defined. Amidst concern that the spirit was weakening, in 2000, a Preamble was added to the Laws instructing all participants to play within the spirit of the game. The Preamble was last updated in 2017, now opening with the line:[\[124]](#cite_note-preamble-126)




> Cricket owes much of its appeal and enjoyment to the fact that it should be played not only according to the Laws, but also within the Spirit of Cricket.


The Preamble is a short statement intended to emphasise the "positive behaviours that make cricket an exciting game that encourages leadership, friendship, and teamwork".[\[125]](#cite_note-2017_changes-127) Its second line states that, "the major responsibility for ensuring fair play rests with the captains, but extends to all players, match officials and, especially in [junior cricket](/wiki/Youth_cricket "Youth cricket"), teachers, coaches and parents".[\[124]](#cite_note-preamble-126)


The umpires are the sole judges of fair and unfair play. They are required under the Laws to intervene in case of dangerous or unfair play or in cases of unacceptable conduct by a player.


Previous versions of the Spirit identified actions that were deemed contrary (for example, appealing knowing that the batter is not out), but all specifics are now covered in the *Laws of Cricket*, the relevant governing playing regulations and disciplinary codes, or left to the judgement of the umpires, captains, their clubs and governing bodies. The terse expression of the Spirit of Cricket now avoids trying to enumerate the diverse cultural conventions that exist in the detail of sportsmanship, or its absence.



Women's cricket
---------------


Main article: [Women's cricket](/wiki/Women%27s_cricket "Women's cricket")


[Mithali Raj](/wiki/Mithali_Raj "Mithali Raj") of India is the highest run scorer in women's international cricket. 


Women's cricket was first recorded in [Surrey](/wiki/Surrey "Surrey") in 1745\.[\[126]](#cite_note-128) International development began at the start of the 20th century, and the first Test match was played between [Australia](/wiki/Australia_Women_cricket_team "Australia Women cricket team") and [England](/wiki/England_women%27s_cricket_team "England women's cricket team") in December 1934\.[\[127]](#cite_note-ICC20C-129) The following year, [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand_Womens_cricket_team "New Zealand Womens cricket team") joined them, and in 2007 [Netherland](/wiki/Netherlands_women_cricket_team "Netherlands women cricket team") became the tenth women's Test nation when they made their debut against [South Africa](/wiki/South_Africa_women_cricket_team "South Africa women cricket team"). In 1958, the International Women's Cricket Council was founded (it merged with the ICC in 2005\).[\[127]](#cite_note-ICC20C-129) In 1973, the first Cricket World Cup of any kind took place when a Women's World Cup was held in England.[\[127]](#cite_note-ICC20C-129) In 2005, the [International Women's Cricket Council](/wiki/International_Women%27s_Cricket_Council "International Women's Cricket Council") was merged with the International Cricket Council (ICC) to form one unified body to help manage and develop cricket. The ICC Women's Rankings were launched on 1 October 2015 covering all three formats of women's cricket. In October 2018 following the ICC's decision to award T20 International status to all members, the Women's rankings were split into separate [ODI](/wiki/One_Day_International "One Day International") (for Full Members) and T20I lists.[\[128]](#cite_note-130)

The [International Cricket Council](/wiki/International_Cricket_Council "International Cricket Council") (ICC), which has its headquarters in [Dubai](/wiki/Dubai "Dubai"), is the global governing body of cricket. It was founded as the Imperial Cricket Conference in 1909 by representatives from England, Australia and South Africa, renamed the International Cricket Conference in 1965 and took up its current name in 1989\.[\[127]](#cite_note-ICC20C-129) The ICC in 2017 has 105 member nations, twelve of which hold full membership and can play [Test cricket](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket").[\[129]](#cite_note-131) The ICC is responsible for the organisation and governance of cricket's major international tournaments, notably the men's and women's versions of the [Cricket World Cup](/wiki/Cricket_World_Cup "Cricket World Cup"). It also appoints the umpires and referees that officiate at all sanctioned Test matches, Limited Overs Internationals and Twenty20 Internationals.


Each member nation has a national cricket board which regulates cricket matches played in its country, selects the national squad, and organises home and away tours for the national team.[\[130]](#cite_note-132) In the West Indies, which for cricket purposes is a federation of nations, these matters are addressed by [Cricket West Indies](/wiki/Cricket_West_Indies "Cricket West Indies").[\[131]](#cite_note-133)

Forms of cricket
----------------


Main article: [Forms of cricket](/wiki/Forms_of_cricket "Forms of cricket")


A [Test match](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") between South Africa and England in January 2005\. The men wearing black trousers are the [umpires](/wiki/Umpire_(cricket) "Umpire (cricket)"). Teams in Test cricket, [first\-class cricket](/wiki/First-class_cricket "First-class cricket") and [club cricket](/wiki/Club_cricket "Club cricket") wear traditional white uniforms and use red [cricket balls](/wiki/Cricket_ball "Cricket ball").


Cricket is a multifaceted sport with multiple formats that can effectively be divided into [first\-class cricket](/wiki/First-class_cricket "First-class cricket"), [limited overs cricket](/wiki/Limited_overs_cricket "Limited overs cricket"), and historically, [single wicket cricket](/wiki/Single_wicket_cricket "Single wicket cricket").


The highest standard is [Test cricket](/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") (always written with a capital "T") which is in effect the international version of first\-class cricket and is restricted to teams representing the twelve countries that are full members of the ICC (see above). Although the term "Test match" was not coined until much later, Test cricket is deemed to have begun with two matches between [Australia](/wiki/Australia_national_cricket_team "Australia national cricket team") and [England](/wiki/England_national_cricket_team "England national cricket team") in the [1876–77 Australian season](/wiki/1876-77_Australian_cricket_season "1876-77 Australian cricket season"); since 1882, most Test series between England and Australia have been played for a trophy known as [The Ashes](/wiki/The_Ashes "The Ashes"). The term "first\-class", in general usage, is applied to top\-level domestic cricket. Test matches are played over five days and first\-class over three to four days; in all of these matches, the teams are allotted two innings each and the [draw](/wiki/Draw_(cricket) "Draw (cricket)") is a valid result.[\[134]](#cite_note-136)


Limited overs cricket is always scheduled for completion in a single day, and the teams are allotted one innings each. There are two main types: [List A](/wiki/List_A_cricket "List A cricket") which normally allows fifty overs per team; and [Twenty20](/wiki/Twenty20 "Twenty20") in which the teams have twenty overs each. Both of the limited overs forms are played internationally as [Limited Overs Internationals](/wiki/Limited_Overs_International "Limited Overs International") (LOI) and [Twenty20 Internationals](/wiki/Twenty20_International "Twenty20 International") (T20I). List A was introduced in England in the 1963 season as a knockout cup contested by the first\-class county clubs. In 1969, a national league competition was established. The concept was gradually introduced to the other leading cricket countries and the first limited overs international was played in 1971\. In 1975, the first [Cricket World Cup](/wiki/Cricket_World_Cup "Cricket World Cup") took place in England. Twenty20 is a new variant of limited overs itself with the purpose being to complete the match within about three to four hours, usually in an evening session. The first [Twenty20 World Championship](/wiki/Twenty20_World_Championship "Twenty20 World Championship") was held in 2007\. In addition, a few full\-member cricket boards have decided to start leagues that are played in the [T10 format](/wiki/T10_cricket "T10 cricket"),[\[135]](#cite_note-137)[\[136]](#cite_note-138)[\[137]](#cite_note-139)[\[138]](#cite_note-140) in which games are intended to last approximately 90 minutes.[\[139]](#cite_note-141)[\[140]](#cite_note-142) Most recently, in 2021, the England and Wales Cricket Board (ECB) introduced a new league featuring a hundred\-ball tournament, known as [The Hundred](/wiki/The_Hundred_(cricket) "The Hundred (cricket)").[\[141]](#cite_note-143) Limited overs matches cannot be drawn, although a [tie](/wiki/Tie_(cricket) "Tie (cricket)") is possible and an unfinished match is a "[no result](/wiki/No-result "No-result")".[\[142]](#cite_note-144)[\[143]](#cite_note-145)


Single wicket was popular in the 18th and 19th centuries, and its matches were generally considered top\-class. In this form, although each team may have from one to six players, there is only one batter in at a time, and that batter must face every delivery bowled while their innings lasts. Single wicket has rarely been played since limited overs cricket began. Matches tended to have two innings per team like a full first\-class one and they could end in a draw.[\[144]](#cite_note-146)



Competitions
------------


Cricket is played at both the international and domestic level. There is one major international championship per format, and top\-level domestic competitions mirror the three main international formats. There are now a number of [T20 leagues](/wiki/T20_leagues "T20 leagues"), which have spawned a "T20 freelancer" phenomenon.[\[145]](#cite_note-147)



### International competitions


Main article: [International cricket](/wiki/International_cricket "International cricket")
Most international matches are played as parts of 'tours', when one nation travels to another for a number of weeks or months, and plays a number of matches of various sorts against the host nation. Sometimes a [perpetual trophy](/wiki/Test_cricket#Perpetual_trophies "Test cricket") is awarded to the winner of the Test series, the most famous of which is [The Ashes](/wiki/The_Ashes "The Ashes").


The ICC also organises competitions that are for several countries at once, including the [ICC Men's Cricket World Cup](/wiki/ICC_Men%27s_Cricket_World_Cup "ICC Men's Cricket World Cup"), [ICC T20 World Cup](/wiki/ICC_T20_World_Cup "ICC T20 World Cup") and [ICC Champions Trophy](/wiki/ICC_Champions_Trophy "ICC Champions Trophy"). A league competition for Test matches played as part of normal tours, the [ICC World Test Championship](/wiki/ICC_World_Test_Championship "ICC World Test Championship"), had been proposed several times, and its [first instance](/wiki/2019%E2%80%9321_ICC_World_Test_Championship "2019–21 ICC World Test Championship") began in 2019\. A league competition for [ODIs](/wiki/One_Day_International "One Day International"), the [ICC Cricket World Cup Super League](/wiki/ICC_Cricket_World_Cup_Super_League "ICC Cricket World Cup Super League"), began in August 2020 and lasted only for one edition. The ICC maintains [Test rankings](/wiki/ICC_Test_Championship "ICC Test Championship"), [ODI rankings](/wiki/ICC_ODI_Championship "ICC ODI Championship") and [T20 rankings](/wiki/ICC_T20I_Championship "ICC T20I Championship") systems for the countries which play these forms of cricket.


Competitions for member nations of the ICC with [Associate status](/wiki/List_of_International_Cricket_Council_members#Associate_members "List of International Cricket Council members") include the [ICC Intercontinental Cup](/wiki/ICC_Intercontinental_Cup "ICC Intercontinental Cup"), for first\-class cricket matches, and the [World Cricket League](/wiki/World_Cricket_League "World Cricket League") for one\-day matches, the final matches of which now also serve as the [ICC World Cup Qualifier](/wiki/ICC_World_Cup_Qualifier "ICC World Cup Qualifier").


The game's only appearance in an [Olympic Games](/wiki/Olympic_Games "Olympic Games") was the [1900 Olympics](/wiki/Cricket_at_the_1900_Summer_Olympics "Cricket at the 1900 Summer Olympics").[\[146]](#cite_note-148) However, it is scheduled to make a return, with the [T20](/wiki/Twenty20 "Twenty20") format of the game, in the [2028 Summer Olympics](/wiki/2028_Summer_Olympics "2028 Summer Olympics") in Los Angeles.[\[147]](#cite_note-149)[\[148]](#cite_note-150)

The world's earliest known cricket match was a [village cricket](/wiki/Village_cricket "Village cricket") meeting in [Kent](/wiki/Kent "Kent") which has been deduced from a 1640 court case recording a "cricketing" of "the Weald and the Upland" versus "the Chalk Hill" at [Chevening](/wiki/Chevening "Chevening") "about thirty years since" (i.e., c. 1611). ### Influence on everyday life


[Street cricket](/wiki/Street_cricket "Street cricket") played in India and the [Dominican Republic](/wiki/Plaquita "Plaquita").
Cricket has had a broad impact on popular culture, both in the [Commonwealth of Nations](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations "Commonwealth of Nations") and elsewhere. It has, for example, influenced the [lexicon](/wiki/Lexicon "Lexicon") of these nations, especially the English language, with various phrases such as "that's not cricket" (that's unfair), "had a good [innings](/wiki/Innings#Usage_outside_of_cricket "Innings")" (lived a long life), and "[sticky wicket](/wiki/Sticky_wicket "Sticky wicket")". "On a sticky wicket" (*aka* "sticky dog" or "glue pot")[\[154]](#cite_note-156) is a [metaphor](/wiki/Metaphor "Metaphor")[\[155]](#cite_note-CalliesKeller2011-157) used to describe a difficult circumstance. It originated as a term for difficult batting conditions in cricket, caused by a damp and soft pitch.[\[156]](#cite_note-Hendrickson2001-158)

Cricket is the subject of works by noted English poets, including [William Blake](/wiki/William_Blake "William Blake") and [Lord Byron](/wiki/Lord_Byron "Lord Byron").[\[157]](#cite_note-art-159) *[Beyond a Boundary](/wiki/Beyond_a_Boundary "Beyond a Boundary")* (1963\), written by Trinidadian [C. L. R. James](/wiki/C._L._R._James "C. L. R. James"), is often named the best book on any sport ever written.[\[158]](#cite_note-160)


In the visual arts, notable cricket paintings include [Albert Chevallier Tayler](/wiki/Albert_Chevallier_Tayler "Albert Chevallier Tayler")'s *[Kent vs Lancashire at Canterbury](/wiki/Kent_vs_Lancashire_at_Canterbury "Kent vs Lancashire at Canterbury")* (1907\) and [Russell Drysdale](/wiki/Russell_Drysdale "Russell Drysdale")'s *[The Cricketers](/wiki/The_Cricketers "The Cricketers")* (1948\), which has been called "possibly the most famous Australian painting of the 20th century."[\[159]](#cite_note-Meacham-161) French [impressionist](/wiki/Impressionism "Impressionism") [Camille Pissarro](/wiki/Camille_Pissarro "Camille Pissarro") painted cricket on a visit to England in the 1890s.[\[157]](#cite_note-art-159) [Francis Bacon](/wiki/Francis_Bacon_(artist) "Francis Bacon (artist)"), an avid cricket fan, captured a batter in motion.[\[157]](#cite_note-art-159) [Caribbean](/wiki/Caribbean "Caribbean") artist [Wendy Nanan](/wiki/Wendy_Nanan "Wendy Nanan")'s cricket images[\[160]](#cite_note-162) are featured in a limited edition first day cover for [Royal Mail](/wiki/Royal_Mail "Royal Mail")'s "World of Invention" stamp issue, which celebrated the London Cricket Conference 1–3 March 2007, first international workshop of its kind and part of the celebrations leading up to the 2007 [Cricket World Cup](/wiki/Cricket_World_Cup "Cricket World Cup").[\[161]](#cite_note-163)


In music, many [calypsos](/wiki/Calypso_music "Calypso music") make reference to the [Sport of Cricket](/wiki/List_of_calypsos_with_sociopolitical_influences#Sports_-_Cricket "List of calypsos with sociopolitical influences").



### Influence on other sports




[Tom Wills](/wiki/Tom_Wills "Tom Wills"), cricketer and co\-founder of [Australian football](/wiki/Australian_football "Australian football")


Cricket has close historical ties with [Australian rules football](/wiki/Australian_rules_football "Australian rules football") and many players have [competed at top levels in both sports](/wiki/List_of_Australian_rules_footballers_and_cricketers "List of Australian rules footballers and cricketers").[\[162]](#cite_note-164) In 1858, prominent Australian cricketer [Tom Wills](/wiki/Tom_Wills "Tom Wills") called for the formation of a "foot\-ball club" with "a code of laws" to keep cricketers fit during the off\-season. The [Melbourne Football Club](/wiki/Melbourne_Football_Club "Melbourne Football Club") was founded the following year, and Wills and three other members codified the first laws of the game.[\[163]](#cite_note-165) It is typically played on [modified cricket fields](/wiki/Australian_rules_football_playing_field "Australian rules football playing field").[\[164]](#cite_note-166)


In England, a number of [association football](/wiki/Association_football "Association football") clubs owe their origins to cricketers who sought to play football as a means of keeping fit during the winter months. [Derby County](/wiki/Derby_County_F._C. "Derby County F. C.") was founded as a branch of the [Derbyshire County Cricket Club](/wiki/Derbyshire_County_Cricket_Club "Derbyshire County Cricket Club") in 1884;[\[165]](#cite_note-167) [Aston Villa](/wiki/Aston_Villa_F._C. "Aston Villa F. C.") (1874\) and [Everton](/wiki/Everton_F._C. "Everton F. C.") (1876\) were both founded by members of church cricket teams.[\[166]](#cite_note-168) [Sheffield United](/wiki/Sheffield_United_F._C. "Sheffield United F. C.")'s [Bramall Lane](/wiki/Bramall_Lane "Bramall Lane") ground was, from 1854, the home of the [Sheffield Cricket Club](/wiki/Sheffield_Cricket_Club "Sheffield Cricket Club"), and then of [Yorkshire](/wiki/Yorkshire_County_Cricket_Club "Yorkshire County Cricket Club"); it was not used for football until 1862 and was shared by Yorkshire and Sheffield United from 1889 to 1973\.[\[167]](#cite_note-169)


In the late 19th century, a former cricketer, English\-born [Henry Chadwick](/wiki/Henry_Chadwick_(writer) "Henry Chadwick (writer)") of [Brooklyn](/wiki/Brooklyn "Brooklyn"), [New York](/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"), was credited with devising the baseball [box score](/wiki/Box_score_(baseball) "Box score (baseball)")[\[168]](#cite_note-170) (which he adapted from the cricket scorecard) for reporting game events. The first box score appeared in an 1859 issue of the *[Clipper](/wiki/New_York_Clipper "New York Clipper")*.[\[169]](#cite_note-NPR-171) The statistical record is so central to the game's "historical essence" that Chadwick is sometimes referred to as "the Father of Baseball" because he facilitated the popularity of the sport in its early days.[\[170]](#cite_note-Myth-172)


The influence of [English sports](/wiki/English_sports "English sports") such as cricket in [British India](/wiki/British_India "British India") resulted in the [local sports](/wiki/Traditional_games_of_South_Asia "Traditional games of South Asia") becoming standardised by the 1920s.[\[171]](#cite_note-173) The 21st century success of the [Indian Premier League](/wiki/Indian_Premier_League "Indian Premier League") also inspired the growth of other sports leagues in India,[\[172]](#cite_note-174) with some of the native sports being further modernised, as with the popular [Pro Kabaddi League](/wiki/Pro_Kabaddi_League "Pro Kabaddi League").[\[173]](#cite_note-175)
