 CEOs Are Bailing Out In Droves; Do They Know Something?
=======================================================


CEO turnover is increasing, adding a new wild card for investors to deal with. (AP, Bloomberg)




 [Licensing](https://www.ibdlicensing.com)

* [MATT KRANTZ](https://www.investors.com/author/krantzm/ "Posts by MATT KRANTZ")
* 11:50 AM ET 12/23/2019





If it feels like there's a CEO revolving door, you're right. CEO turnover in 2019 is at all\-time highs. And musical chairs in the CEO office pose a new challenge for investors.

CEOs Are Bailing Out In Droves; What Do They Know?




[Boeing CEO Dennis Muilenburg was shown the door](https://www.investors.com/news/boeing-ceo-resigns-amid-737-max-crisis-boeing-stock/) Dec. 23 amid criticism of his handling of the 737 Max crisis and scoldings by the FAA for pushing for a quicker return to service for the troubled jet.


And he joins scores of other high\-profile CEO departures this year. **Alphabet** ([GOOGL](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=GOOGL)). **McDonald's** ([MCD](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=MCD)). WeWork. **Under Armour** ([UAA](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=UAA)). **Overstock** ([OSTK](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=OSTK)). **Nike** ([NKE](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=NKE)). All these well\-known companies and hundreds more lost their CEOs this year. The top brass at companies are resigning, retiring, being let go or leaving to "spend more time with the family" at a record clip.


More than 1,400 CEOs left their posts this year through November, [says Challenger, Gray \& Christmas](https://www.challengergray.com/press/press-releases/2019-november-ceo-turnover-report-148-out-november-record-high-continues). That's up 12% from this point last year. And it's the highest January to November total since Challenger started keeping track of the numbers in 2002\. Turnover is even higher than the 1,257 CEOs who exited in 2008 amid the Great Recession.

Turnover hits investors especially hard. More than 280 CEOs from publicly traded firms exited this year through November. And that's already 8% higher than in all of 2018 and the highest level since 2011\.


Ten companies in the broad S\&P 1500 index, which includes small, midsize and large firms, don't even have a permanent CEO right now and [have interim leaders](https://www.investors.com/news/major-ceo-jobs-available-want-make-millions/).


Investors Brace For CEO Change
------------------------------


Such tumult at the top is an issue for investors. A shake\-up at the top of a company can change the thesis on a stock. Management sets a company's strategy and largely controls the elements of [successful CAN SLIM growth investing](https://www.investors.com/ibd-university/can-slim/).


![](opensource/tidytuesday/2021-04-27/images/0.png)


Earnings, new product plans and industry leadership are all in the CEO's hands. Shares of S\&P 500 companies fell 1\.5% in just one day, on average, following news of a CEO departure this year, according to an Investor's Business Daily analysis of data from S\&P Global Market Intelligence.


It's hard for analysts and investors to get perspective on a company when the CEO might bail.


"We're seeing a whole lot more CEO turnover," said Peter Crist, chairman of executive recruitment firm Crist Kolder. "We're seeing a lot of change."


Why Is CEO Turnover Picking Up?
-------------------------------


Just as [employees leave jobs](https://www.investors.com/news/management/leaders-and-success/nurture-employees-to-make-them-more-productive/) for many reasons, explanations for CEOs bailing out abound.


CEOs and companies cited 22 primary reasons for departures this year. "Stepping down," which is more an action than a reason, is the most common, says Challenger, Gray \& Christmas. The term may mean the CEOs are remaining with the company in some capacity, says Andrew Challenger, vice president at Challenger, Gray \& Christmas. That typically means they stick around as a board member or somewhere else in the C\-Suite.


"Especially in startups, we'll see a founder and CEO step down from the CEO role into a chief technology officer or chief development officer (role) as they enter new phases of growth," he said. "Other companies want the former CEO around as a consultant or to guide new leadership through the transition. Companies are hanging on to their institutional knowledge."


Consider online search giant Google's parent, Alphabet. Co\-founder Larry Page announced plans in November to step down from the CEO role he's held since October 2015\. But he's remaining on the board along with fellow co\-founder Sergey Brin. Same with Nike's outgoing CEO Mark Parker. He'll be executive chairman.


Retirement Is Looking Good
--------------------------


Retirement is the No. 2 reason CEOs are leaving. It's a generational changing of the guard.


Many CEOs are at or [nearing traditional retirement age](https://www.investors.com/etfs-and-funds/personal-finance/retirement-concerns-why-you-should-never-retire/), says Fabio Gaertner, associate professor at the Wisconsin School of Business at University of Wisconsin\-Madison. "Generational change is creating more churn," he said. The average age of S\&P 500 CEOs is 58, a point at which many CEOs, especially if successful, might think about packing it in.


Take Michael Polk, 58, who in March announced plans to retire as the CEO of cookware company **Newell Brands** ([NWL](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=NWL)). "I have been privileged to lead Newell Brands for the past eight years," he said in a statement. "I believe now is the right time to transition to the next generation of leadership."


The average age of departing CEOs fell to 59 in 2019, down from 62 in 2018, says Challenger Gray and Christmas.


And generous CEO pay and stock grants make retiring early an option. CEO pay for the largest 500 public companies is up nearly 25% over the last five years, pay firm Equilar says. Median pay for CEOs at large companies surpassed $12 million in 2018\.


[Early retirement might look all the more appealing](https://www.investors.com/etfs-and-funds/retirement/early-retirement-tips-how-fire-financial-independence-retire-early-works/) if CEOs think the 10\-year\-old economic surge might be fading, Crist says. "The CEO looks around and says, 'Well, you know ... I'm 58, my stock is at an all\-time high and we're at the tail end of an economic expansion. Things aren't going to get much better. Time to go.'"


Bull Market Makes Early Retirement An Option
--------------------------------------------


The long\-running bull market makes it more possible for CEOs thinking of retiring to afford it, says James Chyz, associate professor of accounting at the University of Tennessee Knoxville. "The extended bull market, combined with significant amounts of stock compensation, has made many sitting CEOs very wealthy," he said. "Many CEOs are probably deciding that the costs of the job are not worth the incremental increase in their already sizable wealth."


Boomer CEOs are especially looking at retirement seriously, says Matteo Tonello, managing director at The Conference Board. And many have been on the job a long time.


"Thanks in part to a decadelong bull market, CEOs have been staying in their job longer and getting older," he said. The number of S\&P 500 CEOs in their 70s is higher than the number of CEOs under the age of 50, he says. "This factor has been creating the basis for a generational change, which we think will only intensify if the economic expansion ends," Tonello said.



New Demands Pressure CEOs
-------------------------


Walking away from a [multimillion\-dollar salary](https://www.investors.com/etfs-and-funds/retirement/estate-planning-mistakes-rich-people-make/) isn't easy for anyone. But the job is getting harder and taking more of a toll. And that's when the economy is still strong.


"I also suspect that many CEOs foresee a difficult decade ahead as we are almost surely due for a recession. For many CEOs, I imagine the notion of going out on top, much like a star athlete would want, is quite appealing," Chyz said.


Even if the economy's surface is calm and growing, disruption and challenges are going on in many industries, says Dartmouth University business professor Katharina Lewellen, who is about to publish new research on CEO turnover. And even if a company's stock price and profits are headed higher, boards see coming threats, especially from technology. Many would rather put a CEO in place to move the company strategically, rather than try to react later.


"Business is changing," she said. "It might be technologically, or more regulation. ... The CEOs that we have don't stick as well as they used to," she said. Boards are "looking for kind of a better fit."


CEOs usually leave following poor stock performance. WeWork CEO Adam Neumann left the company in September after the company's botched attempt to go public. And **Kraft Heinz** ([KHC](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=KHC)) CEO Bernardo Hees resigned in June. The packaged foods company's stock plunged nearly 60% from the start of his tenure in 2015, while the S\&P 500 gained 41%.


And poor performance stands out even more during a bull market.


CEOs Feel 'Burnout'
-------------------


Rick Belluzo, 65, felt the industry pressure as a CEO firsthand. He's had several stints as CEO at multiple companies after leaving **Microsoft** ([MSFT](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=MSFT)) in May 2002 as its president. In 2016, he left the CEO post of networker **Viavi Solutions** ([VIAV](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=VIAV)), where he's still on the board, and he left video\-editing software company Quantum in 2011\.


"The rate of change has clearly accelerated, and most all industries are going through larger and more frequent transitions driven by changes in technology, business models, and competitive dynamics," he said. "The environment today is unforgiving in terms of executing these changes. Markets are not patient, and the constraints are challenging."


The [pressure can get to the CEO,](https://www.investors.com/news/management/leaders-and-success/follow-through-persistence-see-projects-completion/) Belluzo says. "Sometimes as a leader you don't even understand the level of fatigue until you step back from a situation. It's also really important not to wait too long. I hate to see successful CEOs hold on too long and damage their reputation and the company. In other words, timing is key," he said.


And CEOs are burning out. "These positions are really difficult," Crist said, noting that global demands make the job life\-consuming. "It's a tough gig. You can only do it so long." The average tenure of departing CEOs fell to 10 years in 2019, down from 11 in 2018, says Challenger, Gray \& Christmas.


"You do have people saying, 'Wow, this is really tough,' particularly these big global chairs where you're on an airplane in Asia, South America, Europe," he said. "How long can you do it? And if the stock price is at all\-time high ... now's the time to exit gracefully."



Social Concerns Turn Up Heat On CEOs
------------------------------------


[Investors' intensifying social considerations](https://www.investors.com/research/esg-investing-data-driven-way/) are making life as a CEO even more scrutinized. Call it CEOs' \#MeToo moment.


"This year saw a record number of CEOs who left their post as a result of an investigation related to their personal misconduct," Tonello said. The Conference Board says it found five cases of S\&P 500 CEOs being ousted this year for inappropriate personal behavior, compared to only one in all of the previous five years.


The surprise dismissal of McDonald's CEO Steve Easterbrook was an example. The CEO since 2015 was fired after revelations of a "consensual" relationship with another employee. The firing stung. [Shares of McDonald's lit up](https://www.investors.com/research/mcdonalds-stock-buy-now-mcd-stock/) under Easterbrook's leadership, jumping 96% from his March 1, 2015, start as CEO through his Nov. 3, 2019, termination. During that time the S\&P 500 rose just 46%. McDonald's stock dropped 3% in the first day of trading following the termination.


And similarly, Patrick Byrne, CEO and founder of online retailer Overstock, resigned in August after making controversial political comments.


With [ESG filters part of investing](https://www.investors.com/research/what-is-esg-investing-msci-ratings-provide-answer/), if you make a personal mistake, "You're toast," Crist said. "When a board looks at a CEO, part of their performance review is the judgment of the CEO in terms of ... business judgment (and) personal judgment," he said.


What CEO Turnover Means For Investors
-------------------------------------


Large investors, especially those focused on the long term, are paying attention to rising CEO turnover. But they're not overly concerned.


There's no question a CEO's influence looms large on a company and its stock, says Don Peters, co\-portfolio manager of the $1\.7 billion\-in\-assets T. Rowe Price Diversified Mid\-Cap Growth Fund (PRDMX). "If you point to [one person who is responsible](https://www.investors.com/news/management/leaders-and-success/give-feedback-honest-feedback-hitting-positives-negatives/), it's going to be the CEO."


And disruptions can be jarring. The CEO of one of Peters' holdings, for instance, died this month. Mark Butler, founder and CEO of discount retailer **Ollie's Bargain Outlet** ([OLLI](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=OLLI)), which in recent years has posted outstanding results, unexpectedly passed away at age 61\. John Swygert, 50, shifted to CEO from Ollie's chief operating officer post. T. Rowe's Peters didn't sell the stock though, as the company's strategy will continue.


Investors Price In New CEOs
---------------------------


Reactions to CEO turnover can be severe. Shares of digital storage firm **Western Digital** ([WDC](https://research.investors.com/quote.aspx?symbol=WDC)) dropped 18% in one day following the Oct. 30 announcement that CEO Stephen Milligan was retiring. The stock's drop coincided with the company posting a third\-quarter loss of nearly $200 million. That was the worst one\-day sell\-off of any S\&P 500 company with a CEO change this year.


But while [the CEO is important](https://www.investors.com/tag/leaders-success-profiles/), "It's extremely rare that you are really just betting on the CEO," Peters said, speaking of his own approach. "You're really betting on the team, the company's strategic positioning and the capital allocation."


And focusing on companies, not just the CEO, helps the fund hold shares long term and not react to turnover in the corner office. "If you're investing longer term, you really don't want to have the investment thesis entirely predicated on the CEO's execution, or the CEO being brilliant," he said.


For Peters, "You'd rather own an excellent business with a good or solid CEO versus a poor or marginal business with an A\-plus CEO because usually the business wins."


And data support the idea that investors look beyond just the CEO. Academic studies consistently show stock price reaction to CEO turnover "is either a small positive or indistinguishable from zero," Lewellen said. Meanwhile, companies' profitability tends to improve with a new CEO.


Positive reactions to CEOs being removed is typically a result of relief and hope. Historically, "CEO turnover follows bad stock price performance," she said.


CEO Hot Seat Will Get Hotter
----------------------------


But the forces pushing CEOs out now are different than they were. Investors can handle well\-planned CEO turnover. But sudden departures of key CEOs, especially after a successful run, are harder to deal with. "How do you evaluate Berkshire Hathaway stock after [Warren Buffett retires?](https://www.investors.com/news/management/leaders-and-success/warren-buffetts-net-worth-driven-ironclad-rules/)" professor Gaertner said.


The factors pushing CEOs out are only intensifying, says Eleanor Bloxham, a corporate board governance consulting firm.


"CEOs are going to be held to greater account by the public, by investors, by employees and the board. Technology is going to continue to be an ever\-increasing factor in corporate success," Bloxham said.


"For some CEOs, the CEO job will be less attractive. For some boards the CEO will no longer be a good fit," she said.
